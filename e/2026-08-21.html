🧭 The throughline

Today's market is being run by the long end of the Treasury curve, not by the Fed. A 30-year yield at a 19-year high of 5.32% and a bear-steepening curve say this is a term-premium and fiscal-supply problem — debt crossed $40 trillion on 18 August, interest is now the second-largest budget line, and Bessent's doubled buyback bought less than a day of relief before backfiring into higher yields and two-month-high breakevens. The regime, in short, is fiscal dominance plus a commodity shock: equities have lost the right to treat rates as a tailwind, and the two events that resolve it are Warsh at Jackson Hole and Nvidia on Wednesday.

Auction tails confirm it is a supply-and-compensation story: a $42 billion 10-year note sale cleared at 4.68%, the highest auction stop in 19 years, and the most recent 30-year drew roughly 5.22%, the highest since 2021. A large and underappreciated part of the pressure is private, not public: S&P Global counts about $225 billion of 2026 bond issuance from hyperscalers and related entities such as Nvidia — a 973.7% jump through midyear against a 2020-2024 average of roughly $35 billion a year and $93 billion in 2025.

Bank of America economists estimate that corporate supply plus mortgage-backed issuance has added about 30 basis points to the 10-year yield this year. Demand is visibly tiring: hyperscaler order-book coverage ratios fell from nearly 5x in February to below 2x in July, and Broadcom is reportedly lining up a debt deal of up to $70 billion. Ed Yardeni's framing — that bond vigilantes are stirring globally over government debt — is analyst opinion, but the auction data is not.

Why it matters: if the long end is repricing term premium rather than Fed expectations, then rate cuts would not fix it and could make it worse by adding inflation risk to the same bonds. Watch the next 20- and 30-year auction tails and the bid-to-cover ratios — those are the cleanest real-time read on whether marginal demand for duration is actually there. The AI capex cycle and the federal deficit are now competing for the same finite pool of duration buyers, which is a structurally new constraint on both.

Bessent's buyback bazooka bought the bond market less than 24 hours — and that is the real newsEuronews / CNBC / Treasury

On Wednesday, August 19, Treasury announced it would at least double the size of liquidity-support buybacks for longer-dated nominal coupons, raising the maximum per operation from $2 billion to at least $4 billion beginning September 9. The initial reaction was a textbook relief rally: the 10-year fell more than 5 basis points to 4.647% and the 30-year dropped 9 basis points to 5.196%.

It did not hold. By Thursday the 30-year had risen more than 7 basis points to as much as 5.27%, above where it traded before the announcement, and the 10-year round-tripped 4.68% to 4.63% to 4.71% within 24 hours. Bessent then had to reassure markets a second time, saying the $4 billion ceiling was effectively a floor — "it could be more than the $4 billion per issue" — and that "part of it is signalling here, and to show that we believe that the yields don't reflect the underlying fundamentals." The mechanism matters: buybacks are not money printing.

Treasury funds repurchases of long bonds by issuing elsewhere, mostly bills and shorter coupons, so this is a duration swap — a homemade Operation Twist — not QE. The scale is also modest against the problem: the quarterly buyback envelope is roughly $63 billion in total, of which only about $16 billion touches the 10-to-30-year sector, against a quarterly refunding that raises roughly $28.7 billion of new cash against $96.3 billion of maturing privately held notes and bonds.

Krishna Guha of Evercore ISI called it "a weak form of Operation Twist" that risks backfiring if markets read it as Washington struggling to fund itself cheaply; JPMorgan's Maia Crook warned the intervention "belies underlying structural challenges" and cuts against Treasury's own pledge of regular and predictable issuance.

Why it matters: a failed intervention is more informative than no intervention. It tells you the marginal buyer of 30-year paper is not being deterred by liquidity frictions but by fundamentals — deficits, inflation risk and competing corporate supply — which $4 billion operations cannot fix. The second-order danger is regime perception: if every long-end selloff is met with a bigger buyback, investors will start pricing an informal yield-control regime, which raises the inflation risk premium embedded in the very bonds Treasury is trying to support. Watch whether Treasury escalates past the $63 billion quarterly envelope, and whether it starts shortening the average maturity of issuance — the latter would be the genuine capitulation.

US debt crossed $40 trillion on August 18 — five months after $39 trillion — and interest is now the second-largest line in the budgetCBS / NPR / Axios

Treasury data showed total public debt outstanding topped $40.047 trillion for the first time on August 18, 2026. The pace is the story: the $39 trillion mark was crossed in March, meaning roughly $1 trillion was added in about five months. Interest expense hit $1.17 trillion through the first ten months of fiscal 2026, up 15.5% from the same period of fiscal 2025, and has now overtaken Medicare to become the federal government's second-largest expenditure behind Social Security.

The arithmetic underneath is stark: receipts are running near a record $5 trillion while outlays run near $7 trillion, leaving an annual deficit around $2 trillion; July alone printed a $432 billion deficit. The drivers cited are war spending on Iran, the tax provisions of the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, and the compounding interest bill itself. The Congressional Budget Office projects net interest costs will more than double to roughly $2.1 trillion by 2036, cumulating to about $16 trillion over 2026-2035 — a forecast, not a fact, and one that assumes rates do not go higher than currently modeled.

Bessent's response is that "there's nothing magic about the $40 trillion number... we can grow our way out of that," which is a policy assertion rather than a projection.

Why it matters: the feedback loop is now self-reinforcing. Higher long-end yields raise the cost of rolling roughly a fifth of the debt stock each year, which widens the deficit, which increases issuance, which pushes yields higher still. Because so much of the stock was termed out at 2020-2021 coupons, every maturing bond that reprices from ~2% to ~5% mechanically adds to the interest line regardless of any new spending decision. The falsifier for the pessimistic case is nominal GDP growth persistently exceeding the average interest rate on the debt — if it does, the ratio stabilizes; at a 5.25% 30-year and 3.4% inflation, that is a demanding bar.

Warsh has publicly said the Fed is "not constrained by market prices," and his first Jackson Hole keynote as chair lands Friday, August 28, at a symposium running August 27-29.

Why it matters: the front end is pricing a Fed that is stuck, while the long end is pricing a Fed that may be too tolerant of 3.4% inflation — those two views cannot both be right for long. Warsh's Jackson Hole speech is the single largest scheduled catalyst in the next two weeks, and he has signalled it will be framework-level rather than near-term guidance, which itself is a risk: a chair who declines to validate the market's easing assumptions can steepen the curve further. Watch whether he addresses the Treasury's buyback program at all — any hint that the Fed views it as encroaching on monetary policy would be a genuine institutional story.

Transmission check: mortgages at 6.65%, a soft labor market, cooling inflation — and strategists reaching for the 1987 analogyFreddie Mac / CNBC / BLS

The household channel has already repriced: Freddie Mac's 30-year fixed averaged 6.65% in the week to August 20, down marginally from 6.67%, after touching about 6.69% earlier in the month — the highest since the end of July 2025. Mortgages track the 10-year plus a spread, so a 10-year pinned near 4.7% keeps refinancing dead and locks existing owners into low coupons, which suppresses housing turnover and the consumption that goes with it.

The macro data cuts the other way and is the hawks' problem: July CPI rose just 0.1% month-on-month and 3.4% year-on-year, with core up 0.2% and 2.5% year-on-year, both down a tenth from June, as energy fell 1.5% and pump prices dropped 2.9% — a meaningful cooling from the 4.2% peak in May driven by the Iran conflict's disruption of energy shipping. The labor side is softer still: July nonfarm payrolls fell 23,000 against a consensus of roughly +83,000, with May and June revised down by a combined 103,000.

Weekly claims remain low — 206,000 initial claims in the week to August 15, down 6,000 and below the 210,000 expected, with continuing claims at 1.799 million and an insured unemployment rate of 1.2% — meaning firms are not firing, they are simply not hiring. Strategists are now openly invoking 1987, when the S&P 500 was up more than 35% by August while inflation reaccelerated to 4.2% and bond yields climbed; the parallel drawn is that stocks are richly valued while bonds are cheap on a relative basis, so capital can rotate out of equities purely on the arithmetic of the alternative. That comparison is analyst opinion, not a forecast of a crash.

Why it matters: the Fed is caught between a labor market that argues for cuts and a long end that punishes them. If the September 11 August CPI print comes in soft again while payrolls stay negative, the hawks lose the argument and the front end rallies — but that would steepen the curve further, not flatten it, because the long end is trading fiscal risk, not policy. The clearest falsifier for the whole bearish-duration thesis would be a 30-year auction that stops through with strong indirect (foreign) bidding; the clearest confirmation would be mortgage rates pushing back above 6.75% into a labor market that is already shedding jobs, which is the combination that turns a valuation problem into a credit problem.

Bessent turns the Iran campaign into declared economic war — and the real target list is Tehran's buyersAl Jazeera / NPR / US Treasury

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said on Aug 20 that the US has "a blockade on Iran" and will impose "the toughest sanctions in history," adding that "it is going to work in Iran and we are going to collapse this regime," with a fresh tranche teed up for Monday. The market-relevant mechanism is not the Iran-facing designation list but the secondary threat: Bessent told trading partners they are "either with us or against us," and that anyone moving Iranian money, lifting its oil or supplying seaborne transport will face the full force of Treasury enforcement.

That threat is already visible in flows — China's imports of Iranian crude have fallen to roughly 534,000 b/d in August against an average near 1.4m b/d last year, and Kpler has logged no supertanker crossings of Hormuz carrying Iranian crude since mid-July. Shandong "teapot" refiners, about a fifth of Chinese refining capacity, are both the marginal buyer and the marginal casualty; discounts on sanctioned Iranian barrels have widened from roughly $8/bbl in 2023 to $14–17 now.

Treasury has also attacked the revenue model directly: on July 29 OFAC designated Persian Gulf Marine Insurance Co. and HormuzSafe Marine Services Authority, entities it says brokered IRGC-approved "insurance" that vessels were coerced into buying to transit the strait, partly settled in digital assets, alongside eight shadow-fleet tankers. Bessent's claim that Iranian inflation is running in "triple digits" is an official assertion, not independently verified here.

Tehran is publicly dismissive — parliament speaker Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf called the measures "unjust," and a senior Iranian official told NPR the pivot to economic pressure proves the military campaign failed. Tellingly, Bessent also framed the endgame in price terms — that everyone wants the strait reopened "and for energy prices to come back down" — an admission that the campaign's cost is landing on American consumers as well.

Why it matters: The live question is whether Washington designates an actual Chinese state refiner or bank rather than Hong Kong shells and Shandong teapots. Doing so converts an Iran policy into a US–China financial confrontation with knock-on risk to Treasury demand and the yuan; not doing so caps the campaign's bite, since China absorbs the overwhelming majority of Iran's seaborne crude. Two falsifiers to watch: Chinese lifting of Iranian barrels re-accelerating back above 1m b/d (enforcement is leaking), or Iran conceding on Hormuz in exchange for relief — the single largest downside risk to crude in the current setup.

CENTCOM has escorted 660 million barrels through Hormuz since May — and is still restoring only a third of normal flowCNBC / Quartz / Washington Times

CENTCOM said Friday that escorts under "Project Freedom," announced May 3, have moved more than 660 million barrels of crude and roughly 1,300 commercial vessels through the Strait of Hormuz since early May, with convoys of 15–20 tankers running nightly down a southern channel hugging the Omani coast under Navy and Air Force cover. In the most recent three-week window over 160 million barrels cleared — a run rate above 7m b/d.

That is the number that matters: Hormuz carried roughly 20m b/d of oil liquids pre-war, about a fifth of world consumption, so even a militarily successful convoy operation is restoring only about a third of normal throughput. The strait has been effectively shut since March, after Iranian forces began restricting it on Feb 28. Prices reflect the gap rather than the fix — Brent is around $93.9 after trading near $95 intraday Friday, US regular gasoline is roughly $4.10–4.12/gal and diesel about $5.40, both record seasonal highs, with US gasoline inventories at multiyear lows.

The cost also sits in the insurance stack: additional Gulf war-risk premiums have gone from 1–3% of hull value to 7.5–10%, i.e. $3–10m per voyage on a $100m tanker versus roughly $250,000 pre-hostilities, and Gulf-to-China crude freight has been assessed near $78 per tonne, about four times the pre-conflict five-year average — on the order of $10 a barrel of pure logistics cost.

Meanwhile the USS Abraham Lincoln left the region this week after nine months on station, and the Pentagon has now logged more than 750 US service members wounded since fighting began in late February. Replacement barrels are not arriving smoothly either: dozens of tankers are queued off Venezuela with loading waits stretching to 30 days at José, which handles about 70% of exports, pinning the country near 1.25m b/d.

Why it matters: The escort operation converts a price shock into a quantity-and-cost shock, which is more insidious and more persistent. Every Hormuz barrel now carries a semi-permanent logistics wedge that shows up in Asian refining margins and European product import costs, not only in flat price. Watch whether the Lincoln's departure without an obvious replacement thins convoy frequency — escort tempo, not the sanctions list, now sets the marginal barrel. A single successful strike on a convoyed VLCC would reprice war-risk cover again and is the tail risk that matters more than the Brent headline.

Ukraine hits a refinery 1,500 km inside Russia — but Russian crude exports are rising, not fallingKyiv Independent / Moscow Times / CREA

Overnight Friday Ukrainian drones struck the Lukoil-Permnefteorgsintez refinery in Perm, roughly 1,500 km from the border, along with the Marinovka airbase in Volgograd region; Zelensky called the strikes "our long-range sanctions," and Russia's defence ministry claimed 325 drones downed overnight. The cumulative damage to refining is real and measurable: Russian refinery runs fell to about 3.6m b/d in July, the lowest since May 2002 and roughly a third below seasonal norms, per Bloomberg-cited data, after a campaign that has reached Syzran, Omsk and now the Urals.

Moscow has banned gasoline exports since late March and is weighing a diesel and kerosene ban, with rationing and queues reported across several regions. But the second-order effect runs the other way — crude that cannot be processed at home gets exported, and seaborne crude shipments from Russian ports have run near 3.3–3.4m b/d through July and August. CREA's July analysis put Russian crude export revenue roughly flat month-on-month at about €392m a day, with pipeline earnings down 21% offset by a 7% rise in seaborne receipts; note that data providers currently disagree sharply on the Urals differential, so treat any single discount figure with caution.

The clearest evidence of who is arbitraging this: Saudi Arabia took about 1.1m tonnes of Russian fuel oil and vacuum gasoil in July — roughly 46% of Russia's seaborne exports of those grades — burning discounted Russian heavy fuel for summer air-conditioning load so it can sell its own crude into a $94 market, while India took zero (against 150k tonnes in June) under US tariff pressure and Singapore/Malaysia flows rose 2.5x to 470k tonnes.

Ukraine is paying a symmetrical price at home: Russia has damaged or destroyed more than 300 Ukrainian filling stations in six months, energy minister Denys Shmyhal told parliament Friday, with 4.8 GW of generation repaired and three of nine operating reactor units in maintenance heading into winter.

Why it matters: Ukraine's strike campaign is best read as an attack on refining margin rather than on Kremlin cash — destroying crack spreads at home pushes crude offshore, where the price cap is leaky and the shadow fleet already carries 60–80% of exports. That is why it raises global diesel and gasoline prices (bad for US and EU headline inflation) more reliably than it cuts Russian revenue. The signal to watch is a Russian diesel and kerosene export ban: it would tighten an already record-tight global distillate market just as northern-hemisphere heating demand ramps, and is the precise mechanism by which a drone over Perm shows up in a New Jersey diesel rack price.

Ukraine's Agriculture Ministry has cut its 2026/27 agricultural export forecast to about 29.6m tonnes from an earlier 64.4m — a 54% reduction — with wheat exports seen down 53% to 8.3m tonnes, after a sustained Russian campaign against the Odesa port complex. The concentration risk is the entire story: more than 90% of Ukraine's farm exports move through three deepwater Odesa-area ports that were loading roughly 7m tonnes of grain a month before the campaign began.

Russian missile and drone attacks targeted 57 Ukrainian and foreign-flagged merchant vessels in July alone, and Forbes Ukraine reported Friday that 52 civilian ships have been struck over the past six weeks; commercial arrivals at the deepwater ports have effectively stopped since July 22 — the peak of the wheat harvest — with 30–40% of scheduled July–August calls cancelled and freight into and out of Ukraine roughly doubled.

Kyiv is striking the mirror-image chokepoint: loadings at all three major Novorossiysk terminals have been suspended following Ukrainian drone attacks, and Ukraine hit a Russian Black Sea grain terminal on Aug 13. Prices have moved less than the volume loss implies — CBOT wheat is around $6.70/bu after a two-year peak near $7.06 in July, and MATIF milling wheat about €225/t — because Russia and Ukraine together still account for close to 30% of projected 2026/27 world wheat exports and the market is pricing this as a logistics disruption rather than a crop failure.

Former Trump envoy Keith Kellogg was shown port damage in Odesa on Friday, a signal Washington now treats the export corridor as a negotiating asset, and Zelensky raised food security directly with the UN Secretary-General the same day.

Why it matters: The pain lands on import-dependent buyers rather than on Chicago: Egypt, Bangladesh, Indonesia and North African importers who bought Ukrainian wheat at a freight advantage now pay up for Russian, French or Australian origin plus higher war-risk cover. Two things to watch: whether marine war-risk underwriters withdraw Black Sea cover outright — that would close the corridor faster than any missile — and whether unshippable harvest collapses domestic Ukrainian farmgate prices. Agriculture is Ukraine's largest export earner, so a 54% cut is a balance-of-payments and budget-financing event, not merely a commodity story.
