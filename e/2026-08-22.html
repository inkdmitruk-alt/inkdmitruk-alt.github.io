The week's defining move was at the long end. The 30-year Treasury yield touched 5.327% intraday on Tuesday 18 August, its highest level since 2007 — a 19-year high — and stayed above 5.3% for most of the week. The 10-year reached 4.748%, the highest since January 2025. The 2-year barely moved, sitting around 4.175% and finishing one session down less than a basis point. That combination — long yields up, front end pinned — is a bear steepener, and it widened the 2s30s spread to roughly 114 basis points.

The distinction matters: a front end that refuses to sell off means the market is not repricing the Fed's policy path; it is repricing term premium, the extra compensation demanded for holding duration against fiscal supply and inflation risk. This is not a US-only phenomenon, which weakens the case that it is purely a Washington story: the 30-year Bund hit 3.763%, a 15-year high, the French 30-year reached its highest since 2008, and the 30-year JGB traded around 4.13%.

Friday brought no relief — longer-dated yields rose again as the buyback-driven rally faded. CNBC floated a comparison to 1987, when a bond rout preceded an equity break; treat that as an analyst analogy, not a base case, since the 1987 setup involved a far more violent yield move over a shorter window.

Why it matters: a bear steepener with an anchored front end is the market pricing fiscal and inflation risk, not recession — which means Fed cuts would not fix it and could make it worse by steepening further. Watch the 2s30s spread and the 30-year auction tails: if the curve keeps steepening while the 2-year holds near 4.17%, the message is that no plausible monetary policy path resolves the supply problem.

US debt crossed $40 trillion — doubled in under a decade, with annual interest now above $1 trillion and a projected $2.1 trillion FY2026 deficitFiscal

Treasury data published on 19 August showed total outstanding US debt passing $40 trillion for the first time, having roughly doubled since 2017. The number that actually binds is not the stock but the flow: interest on the debt now costs more than $1 trillion a year, and the CBO projects a $2.1 trillion deficit for fiscal 2026. The New York Times attributes the acceleration to three specific pressures that all landed at once — spending on the Iran war, the tax cuts, and tariff refunds now flowing back to companies after the Supreme Court rejected the levies, an unbudgeted reversal of what had been counted as revenue.

The arithmetic of the rollover is what the bond market is pricing: on a $40 trillion stock, each percentage point of higher average interest cost is worth roughly $400 billion a year once the debt fully turns over, and the refinancing is happening into a 30-year yield above 5.3% rather than the sub-2% coupons being retired. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent's response was to downplay the level — he said there is 'nothing magic about the $40 trillion number,' argued the US can 'grow our way' out, and claimed a 'very good chance' the deficit has already peaked under Trump.

Those are assertions, not projections from an independent scorekeeper, and the CBO's $2.1 trillion figure is the counterweight. On Capitol Hill both parties spent the week lamenting the milestone while conceding no mechanism exists to act on it before the November midterms.

Why it matters: the debt level is a slow variable, but the interest bill is a fast one — it crowds out discretionary spending inside the same budget window that new borrowing has to be sold into. Watch the next quarterly refunding announcement for the maturity mix: if Treasury shortens issuance to dodge the 5.3% long end, it trades term-premium relief today for a much larger, faster-repricing rollover wall later.

Verdict on the buyback experiment: it worked for exactly one session, then backfired — long yields rose again and breakeven inflation hit a two-month highTreasury

On Wednesday 19 August, Treasury announced it would at least double the size of its debt repurchase operations from September, deliberately targeting the longer-duration part of the curve. The first-day result looked like success: long yields pulled back from multi-decade highs, the S&P rose modestly, and risk assets ripped — bitcoin jumped from around $64,000 to above $68,000 with roughly $1.4 billion of shorts liquidated, and spot bitcoin ETFs took $517 million of net inflows, their largest in three and a half months.

It lasted a day. On Thursday the rout restarted, the Dow fell about 700 points, and Bessent doubled down publicly, saying Treasury could intervene again and that 'we have a big tool kit.' By Friday longer-dated yields were rising once more and the buyback rally had fizzled outright. The tell is in inflation expectations: the breakeven rate hit its highest level in more than two months, meaning the market read a Treasury operation designed to cap long yields as debasement rather than stabilisation — buybacks funded by more bill issuance shorten the government's effective maturity and look, to a bond investor, like fiscal-side duration suppression.

The cross-asset confirmation was unambiguous: the dollar weakened, gold rebounded, and bitcoin ran about 25% in days to nearly $80,000, trading at $77,019 as of Saturday midday (-0.7% over 24 hours) with ether at $2,411.54. Ray Dalio's reading — that the buyback announcement fits a pattern signalling an approaching debt crisis, and that investors should overweight gold and bitcoin rather than bonds — is his opinion, but the price action across four uncorrelated markets moved in exactly the direction his framework predicts.

Why it matters: a Treasury that manages duration is doing something functionally close to QE without the Fed's mandate or independence, and the market has now demonstrated it will charge an inflation premium for it rather than grant a discount. Watch breakevens and the dollar as the scorecard: if Treasury upsizes buybacks again in September and breakevens widen further while the dollar falls, the operation is actively raising the cost of capital it was meant to lower.

Who wins and who loses is unusually clean. Losers: anyone buying or refinancing a home, students financing college on rate-linked loans, and small businesses on floating credit. Winners: savers, with 5.00% APY still available on high-yield deposits, and retirees, with TIPS yields at or near 20-year highs offering a real return that has not been available since the mid-2000s. The anomaly worth flagging is the dollar: it weakened during a week when US yields rose, inverting the normal capital-attraction relationship.

When a currency and its bond market fall together, the market is charging a sovereign risk premium rather than rewarding higher real returns — the same signal that pushed gold higher on the week.

Why it matters: the housing channel is where a term-premium shock stops being a market story and becomes a political one, roughly ten weeks before the midterms — and Fannie's revision implies affordability gets worse, not better, through year-end. Watch the mortgage-to-Treasury spread and the dollar together: a widening spread plus a falling dollar would mean the risk premium is spreading from the sovereign into the mortgage market, which is exactly what buyback intervention was supposed to prevent.

Iran pre-attacks the legal basis of the next US sanctions tranche as Brent closes the week near $94 with Hormuz still rationedIran / oil

Washington's next sanctions package on Iran arrives with the Strait of Hormuz effectively shut since early March, and Tehran spent Saturday attacking not the substance but the jurisdiction: Iranian officials publicly condemned US "extraterritorial sovereignty," which is the doctrine underpinning secondary sanctions — penalties on third-country banks, refiners, ports and shipowners rather than on Iranian entities directly.

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has framed the package explicitly as a substitute for combat, telling CNBC the measures will likely negate the need for major US military operations, after earlier promising the "toughest sanctions in history" and telling allies they must decide whether they are with the US or against it. Tehran is not folding: President Masoud Pezeshkian said Friday the world had "approved our victory" — rhetoric rather than a negotiating position, but a signal that delay is itself the Iranian strategy, a point The Hill's own opinion pages made this morning in arguing the US is measuring the wrong variable.

Markets are pricing standoff, not resolution: Brent settled just under $94/bbl on Friday (roughly $94.24, the highest since July) for a second consecutive weekly gain of about 6%, with WTI just below $87 and up around 5% on the week. The critical transmission is volume, not headline price — CENTCOM says it has escorted 660 million barrels through Hormuz since early May, roughly 6 mb/d against a pre-war throughput near 20 mb/d, meaning the strait now works as a rationed, convoy-dependent corridor rather than a closed one.

That structure converts a supply story into a logistics-and-insurance tax: freight, war-risk premia and demurrage absorb the difference, and the barrels that do move arrive slower and dearer. Winners are Atlantic-basin crude producers, Gulf exporters with bypass capacity (Saudi Arabia's East-West line to Yanbu, ADNOC's Fujairah pipeline) and tanker owners collecting war-risk rates; losers are Asian refiners paying the freight and any buyer of Iranian barrels now facing designation risk.

China, which historically absorbed roughly 90% of Iran's crude exports, is the real target of the package even though it is not the named subject.

Why it matters: the untested question is whether Treasury designates genuinely sovereign-adjacent counterparties — Chinese teapot refiners, terminal operators or mid-tier banks — rather than the usual shell traders and shadow-fleet vessels. That would be the first real state-level secondary sanction of this campaign, and the moment the oil curve has to price an actual supply loss instead of a logistics tax; a designation of a Chinese port would also invite retaliation in rare earths or agricultural purchases. Watch the designation list itself, the $100 Brent line, and whether Trump's claim that he views Hormuz as "American territory" hardens into a formal convoy regime — the marine insurance market reprices off codified escort rules, not off statements.

The clearest second-order effect of the Iran war is not in crude but in European gas, because Qatari LNG loads inside the Gulf and has no pipeline workaround. TTF held above €65/MWh on Friday, the highest since January 2023, with Ukrainian coverage citing a roughly 120% rise across EU hubs this year; Asian and European benchmarks moved together, with weekly assessments putting TTF/JKM around $20.8/MMBtu on 14 August versus $18.8 a week earlier, after Hormuz transits fell sharply.

The aggravating factor is inventory: EU storage sits near 62% full, the lowest seasonal level in records going back to 2009, with roughly five weeks of injection season left before the heating season begins — meaning Europe must either buy at these prices or enter winter structurally short. The mechanism is a bidding war: stranded Qatari cargoes force European utilities to outbid Asian buyers for the same Atlantic and US Gulf volumes, which compresses the TTF-JKM spread and pulls flexible US cargoes east and west simultaneously.

Forecast, not fact: at least one consultancy signalled it will raise its European gas forecast in September toward an average near €60/MWh for Q4 2026 and Q1 2027, up from €45/MWh currently. The economic logic is precise: Russian crude exports are already capped and discounted by sanctions, so refining is where the marginal fiscal value sits — knocking out distillation capacity forces Moscow either to export more crude at a wider Urals discount or to run domestic fuel shortfalls, and product export bans plus regional rationing have been the recurring response. Novokuibyshevsk is a repeat target that has not returned to full capacity since an April strike, which matters more than any single fire: cumulative, unrepaired outages compound because turbine, catalyst and heat-exchanger replacements are themselves sanctioned imports.

Analyst view, not fact: the consensus among Russia-watchers cited by the Kyiv Independent is a prolonged bout of stagflation, with 2026 growth unlikely to exceed 1% and inflation stuck at or above 6% — a macro backdrop that makes fuel-price spikes politically expensive. The losers are Rosneft and the federal budget's oil-and-gas revenue line; the beneficiaries are non-Russian product exporters into Central Asia and the Caucasus, and, at the margin, global diesel cracks.

Why it matters: this is the one theatre where Ukraine can impose costs faster than Western sanctions committees can. The measurable outputs are Russian domestic wholesale diesel and gasoline prices on the St Petersburg exchange, the length and scope of any renewed product export ban, and the Urals-to-Brent discount, which widens whenever refining goes down and crude has to clear instead. Watch also whether the Ozon strike marks a deliberate widening of target sets from military-adjacent energy to consumer logistics — that would change the insurance and staffing calculus for Russian commercial operators far from the front, and invites symmetrical Russian targeting of Ukrainian retail and distribution assets, which is already happening (the Aurora distribution hub was hit on 22 August).
