📊 Macro & Rates 5 items

One speech moved September from a 35% tail risk to a coin flip — and futures are now more hawkish than the prediction marketsFed pricing

Before Kevin Warsh spoke at Jackson Hole on Friday, fed funds futures put roughly a 35% probability on a September hike and about 70% on another hold; by the close, CME FedWatch showed 54-59% depending on the snapshot, with CNBC citing "nearly 60%" and Yahoo Finance pegging it at 57%. The trigger was two lines: "We must be confident that underlying inflation is moving to our objective, clearly and at sufficient speed.

Otherwise, we have work to do," plus the assertion that short-term rates remain the Fed's "predominant tool" and that financial conditions are not restrictive. The mechanical transmission was immediate — the 2-year Treasury went from 4.22% to 4.30% during the speech and closed at 4.34%, roughly 8bp higher on the day. Note the venue split: fed funds futures repriced to 54-59% while Kalshi sat at 48% and Polymarket at 49%, meaning the rates market is materially more hawkish than the retail-facing prediction venues — a gap that historically closes toward futures.

The starting point matters: the target range is 3.50-3.75%, unchanged all year after three cuts in late 2025, so a hike would be the first tightening of this cycle, not a pause reversal. Societe Generale (forecast, not fact) now models 25bp increases in September, December and March 2027. Skeptics pushed back — Michael Arone of State Street argued markets are overreacting because "the economy is not firing on all cylinders," while Jeffrey Roach of LPL called the speech "distinctly hawkish." Alan Blinder, former Fed vice chair, said it "sounded to me like somebody who thought interest rates should go up."

Why it matters: the entire 2026 rates complex was built on a Fed that would hold and eventually cut; a live hike inverts the direction of the first derivative for every duration-sensitive asset. What to watch: whether the futures-vs-prediction-market gap (54-59% vs 48-49%) converges upward or collapses after Friday's payrolls, and whether any FOMC speaker walks the message back before the September 15-16 meeting.

The curve flattened as a credibility verdict: front end up 8bp, long end finished the week lower, 2s10s down to roughly 38bpCurve

The most informative move of the week was not the level of yields but their shape. Friday's action was a textbook bear-flattener: the 2-year rose about 8bp to 4.34%, the 10-year added 4bp to 4.72-4.73%, and the 30-year gained just 2bp to 5.21% — yet across the full week the 10-year finished roughly 2bp LOWER even as the front end jumped. By my arithmetic on those closes, 2s10s narrowed to about 38-39bp.

This is the exact mirror image of what happened after the July 29 FOMC, when the Fed held 9-3 and investors dumped long bonds, driving the 30-year up as much as 14bp to nearly 5.23%, a 19-year high, while short rates fell — a bear steepener that Ben Emons of Highline Asset Management read as the market saying Warsh's "policy strategy lacks credibility," and that Evercore framed as ground Warsh had to make up.

Friday reversed that: the long end is now willing to price a lower terminal path precisely because the front end is pricing a hike. Winners and losers follow directly — banks get a worse carry trade as the curve flattens, and the Russell 2000 fell 1.48% on the week against a Nasdaq up 0.85%, since small caps carry floating-rate debt and are the purest front-end sensitivity in equities.

Households pay too: the Freddie Mac 30-year fixed averaged 6.66% on August 27, up from 6.65% the prior week and 6.56% a year ago, with the 15-year at 5.98% — a long end anchored near 5.21% on the 30-year keeps mortgages sticky regardless of what the Fed does in September. Seema Shah of Principal noted investors reward "policy clarity, even when that clarity carries a more hawkish message" — which is what the flattening is measuring.

Why it matters: a flattening curve on a hawkish signal means the bond market believes the Fed will act, which is the opposite of July's "behind the curve" steepening and is the single best real-time gauge of Warsh's credibility. What to watch: if the 30-year pushes back above 5.23% while the 2-year rises too, the market is saying a hike is insufficient rather than sufficient — that combination, not the level of the 10-year, is the stress signal.

The September collision: core PCE stuck at 3.3% against payrolls that fell 23,000 — the Fed would be hiking into a shrinking labour marketSeptember setup

What makes September genuinely uncertain is that the two halves of the mandate are pointing in opposite directions with unusual force. On inflation, the July PCE released August 26 showed headline at 3.7% year-over-year and core at 3.3%, unchanged from June, both up 0.2% on the month — and Warsh added the detail that 54% of tracked goods and services rose 3% or more over the past year, against 32% in the two decades before the pandemic, which is his argument that the breadth of inflation, not just the level, has changed.

On employment, July nonfarm payrolls unexpectedly FELL by 23,000 against a Dow Jones consensus of +83,000, government shed 53,000 jobs, leisure and hospitality lost about 40,000, and average hourly earnings growth slipped to 3.2% year-over-year, the weakest since May 2021; the unemployment rate ticked down to 4.1% only because the labour force shrank. Warsh's resolution of the conflict was explicit — he said the inflation data "are more concerning" than the job market, where unemployment remains low.

The committee is already split: on July 29 the FOMC held 9-3, with Beth Hammack, Neel Kashkari and Lorie Logan all dissenting in favour of a hike, the first time since September 2016 that three officials dissented in the same direction, which Warsh called "a good family fight." That vote is why the September bar is lower than the odds alone suggest — three votes are already there.

The political overlay is live too: Trump appointed Warsh in May and has spent two years demanding cheaper credit, so a hike would be his own pick tightening into a midterm year with debt costs and prices already a campaign liability. Citi (forecast, not fact) expects unemployment above 4.5% within months, which would make a September hike look badly timed in hindsight.

Why it matters: this is a rare setup where the Fed may tighten into falling employment — the policy error risk is symmetric and large in both directions, which is exactly why the front end moved 8bp on rhetoric alone. What to watch: whether a second consecutive negative payroll print on September 4 is enough to override 3.3% core PCE, and whether any of the three July dissenters signals a change of mind in the pre-meeting window.

Nvidia's reported $12.9B for Hugging Face turns the open-weight layer into the hottest acquisition class in the ValleyM&A

Nvidia has reportedly agreed to buy Hugging Face for about $12.9B — roughly $13B in some accounts — which would be by far its largest acquisition ever, dwarfing the $6.9B it paid for Mellanox in 2020. The price is the story: Hugging Face runs at roughly $150M of annualized revenue, so the deal is being struck at something near 85x sales, and at nearly three times the $4.5B valuation set by its $235M round in 2023.

It is not an isolated bid — Nvidia has already agreed to take Poolside at about $6B with most employees moving over, Stripe has bought model-router OpenRouter at a $7B-plus valuation, and Fireworks, which routes roughly 40 trillion tokens a day, is openly discussed as the next target. The mechanism is defensive vertical integration: as OpenAI and Google build their own inference silicon, Nvidia is paying to own the distribution layer where developers pick models, so that the default path still runs on CUDA; Stripe's framing is that tokens are becoming "the central currency" of AI-built companies and it wants the toll booth.

The valuation gap against fundamentals is the tell — only about 6% of companies actually use open-weight models today, so buyers are paying for a future share of inference, not current revenue. Nvidia's run is broader than this one deal: it acquired foundation-model startup Kumo in June and is reported to be in early talks with Korean chip designer Rebellions about a partnership, investment or acquisition.

Why it matters: a chipmaker paying ~85x sales for a software distribution asset is the clearest signal yet that the constraint in AI is shifting from who makes the accelerator to who owns the developer default — and it means Nvidia's moat spending is now showing up in M&A, not just capex. What to watch: whether the Hugging Face deal is formally confirmed (it is still sourced to reports, not a filing) and how antitrust reviewers treat a GPU monopolist buying the neutral model registry the whole ecosystem publishes to; whether Fireworks or another router transacts next, which would confirm the category re-rating; and whether Nvidia's balance sheet appetite starts competing with its own customers' financing needs. Opinion, labeled: if regulators force Hugging Face to stay vendor-neutral, most of the strategic value Nvidia is paying for evaporates.

Friday's other deal: Lambda borrows another $1B for GPUs — the buildout's split between hyperscalers paying cash and neoclouds paying interestDeals and financing

On Friday, neocloud Lambda closed $1B of private, short-dated debt arranged by JPMorgan Chase, earmarked for Nvidia chips that it will lease to Microsoft — the short tenor is itself the thesis, implying Lambda expects to deploy the hardware and generate lease revenue fast enough to retire the paper. It is the fourth financing in ten months: a $1.5B equity round at a $5.43B valuation in November 2025, a $1B secured credit facility in May 2026, a $926M senior secured term loan earlier this week specifically for GB300 GPUs, and now this — with a reported $3B pre-IPO round in discussion on top.

The macro frame TechCrunch attaches is the number to hold onto: banks and tech companies have raised over $400B of AI-related debt globally so far in 2026. Contrast that with the demand side announced during Nvidia's earnings call: AWS committed to deploy roughly 2 million additional Nvidia GPUs — Blackwell Ultra, Rubin and Rubin Ultra — across 2027 and 2028, on top of the 1 million it agreed to in March, effectively tripling the order.

Financial terms were not disclosed, but at prevailing unit costs the AWS commitment runs to tens of billions of dollars, funded from operating cash flow rather than secured loans. That is the fault line in the buildout: Amazon buys capacity with earnings, Lambda rents it with leverage, and the two are exposed to completely different things if the cost of money moves. Nvidia, meanwhile, is quietly widening the moat beyond the die itself — TechCrunch's Saturday piece argues the new data-center generation wins on traffic control and system efficiency rather than raw processor cycles, which favors buyers of full racks over buyers of loose chips.

Why it matters: this is where Warsh's Friday hawkishness actually bites the AI trade — not through Nvidia's P/E, but through the roughly $400B of floating-and-refinancing AI debt sitting under the neoclouds, where a September hike raises the carry on exactly the short-dated paper Lambda just issued. What to watch: credit spreads on AI-linked debt and whether the next neocloud raise prices wider; Lambda's pre-IPO round and any IPO filing, which would put a public mark on the leased-GPU model; and GPU depreciation schedules across the neoclouds, since short-dated debt against assets being depreciated over five or six years only works if utilization stays near full. Forecast, labeled as such: expect the hyperscaler-vs-neocloud funding spread to become the main way this market differentiates AI infrastructure names into year-end.
