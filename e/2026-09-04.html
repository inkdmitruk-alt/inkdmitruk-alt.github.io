August payrolls blew out at +162k vs 53k expected with +55k in revisions — and the market immediately repriced a September Fed HIKE to ~60%Jobs

The BLS reported nonfarm payrolls up 162,000 in August against a 53,000 Dow Jones consensus — more than five times the prior 12-month average of just 31,000 a month — and the revisions were arguably the bigger shock: June went from +20k to +31k and July flipped from -23k to +21k, adding 55,000 jobs and erasing the "labor market is cracking" narrative that had suppressed hike pricing since the July 7 miss.

Unemployment held at 4.1% (7.0 million unemployed), participation ticked up to 61.6% and the employment-to-population ratio was flat at 59.1%, so the jobless rate stayed put for the healthy reason — more people entering, more people hired. The composition is softer than the headline: food services added 59,000 and government education 42,000, while health care contributed only 13,000 (well below its historical pace) and information shed 23,000, so the beat leans on low-wage services and seasonal public-sector hiring rather than cyclical private strength.

Average hourly earnings rose $0.10 to $37.75, +0.3% m/m and +3.1% y/y — below headline PCE of 3.7%, meaning real wages are still shrinking roughly 0.6pp a year even as the payroll count booms. Futures pricing for a 25bp hike at the September 15-16 FOMC jumped to about 60% from 49% the previous day, which would take the target range from 3.50-3.75% to 3.75-4.00%. Equities read it as good-news-is-bad-news: the Dow fell about 300 points, the S&P 500 sat at 7,723 (-0.32%), the Nasdaq at 26,505 (-0.30%) and bitcoin dropped to $79,536 (-1.6%).

Winners: cash, T-bills, banks with asset-sensitive books, the dollar's carry appeal. Losers: long-duration equities, mortgage borrowers, and anyone who bought the September-hold trade after Waller spoke on Thursday.

Why it matters: with core PCE at 3.3% and the funds midpoint at 3.625%, the real policy rate is barely +0.3% — hawks can now argue the Fed is not restrictive at all AND the labor market can take a hike, which is the exact combination that makes a September move live. What to watch: the August CPI on September 11 at 8:30 ET is the last major data point before the FOMC; also watch whether the food-services/government-education mix repeats or reverses in the September report, since a payback month would take the pressure straight back off.

The 2-year yield pushed to its highest level since January 2025 on the payrolls print, having already touched 4.36% intraday on September 1; it closed around 4.35% on September 3, so the front end has now made three separate multi-year highs in a week. The long end had already moved first — from the missed week, the 10-year closed August at its highest since January 2025 and then hit its highest level since November 2023 on September 2, around 4.78-4.79%, while the 30-year has been parked near 5.25% after topping 5.33% on August 18, a 19-year high.

That leaves 2s10s at roughly +43bp and 2s30s near +90bp: a positively sloped, bear-flattening-at-the-front curve, which is the classic shape when the market prices a central bank tightening into inflation it does not believe will be beaten. Crucially, the long end is being held up by supply and risk premium, not by optimism — heavy federal borrowing, a wave of AI-related corporate issuance competing for the same duration dollars, and policy uncertainty are the named culprits, which is why a hawkish Fed has not pulled 30-year yields down the way a credible inflation-fighting Fed normally would.

The trigger for the early-September leg was mechanical: Brent crude pushed above $92 as strikes in the Iran conflict resumed, reviving an energy-inflation impulse, and the August ISM manufacturing survey showed prices paid stuck at 71.1 (consensus 70.5) even as the headline PMI slipped to 54.6 from 55.6. Winners: money-market funds, floating-rate lenders, and anyone short duration.

Losers: 30-year mortgage applicants (rates track the long end, not the funds rate), regional bank AFS portfolios, REITs, utilities, and the Treasury's own interest bill.

Why it matters: the administration wants lower mortgage rates before the midterms, but the part of the curve that sets mortgage rates is the part the Fed controls least — a September hike could flatten the curve and leave 30-year mortgage costs roughly where they are. What to watch: whether 2s10s starts flattening toward zero as hike odds firm (a recession signal returning), the 30-year's 5.33% August high as the line that defines a fresh long-end breakout, and Brent's path around $92, since energy is doing much of the work in this repricing.

From the missed week: Waller publicly broke with Warsh on September 3, knocking ~12 points off hike odds in a day and exposing a genuine split at the top of the FOMCFed split

On Thursday, September 3 — inside the week you missed — Fed Governor Christopher Waller said he would favor holding rates steady in September if the August CPI shows inflation continuing to cool, an explicit conditional that markets read as the first senior pushback against Chair Kevin Warsh's hawkish Jackson Hole debut on August 28. Waller's substantive argument matters more than the vote count: he said the tariff pass-through into consumer prices has already largely happened, that higher energy costs since the Middle East war have not broadly spilled into other categories, and that underlying inflation is moderating — in other words, he is treating the current 3%-handle inflation as a level shift already in the price index rather than a live acceleration.

The market reaction was immediate and large: September hike odds fell roughly 12 percentage points to about 54.6% on CME FedWatch, having run as high as 66% on August 31 after the Warsh-driven repricing. Risk assets took it as a green light — bitcoin reclaimed $80,000 and US spot bitcoin ETFs saw $731 million of inflows on September 3, their largest day since January, with analysts attributing the flow directly to Waller.

Friday's payrolls then partly undid it, pushing odds back to roughly 60%, so the September meeting is now genuinely two-sided rather than a done deal. The practical read: Warsh has the chair and the hawkish framing, Waller has laid down a public marker for dissent, and the deciding variable has been outsourced to a single data release. Opinion/interpretation, not reported fact: a Warsh hike over a Waller dissent would be an unusually contentious first real decision for a new chair, and dissent risk is itself a reason some desks are fading full hike conviction.

Why it matters: a visible Warsh-vs-Waller split means the September decision carries communication risk on top of policy risk — a hike with dissent, or a hold after hawkish rhetoric, both damage the forward-guidance channel the Fed relies on. What to watch: any further FOMC speakers before the pre-meeting blackout, whether other governors align with Waller's tariff-pass-through-is-done framing, and the dissent line in the September 16 statement at 2:00pm ET.

That distinction is the entire argument inside the FOMC: Waller reads a stall caused by one-off tariff and energy level shifts as something to look through, while Warsh reads 3.3% core with a 3.50-3.75% funds rate as a real policy rate of only about +0.3%, which is close to neutral and arguably not restrictive at all.

The forward-looking price indicators from the missed week argue for the hawks: the August ISM manufacturing survey showed prices paid at 71.1, above the 70.5 consensus and unchanged from July, meaning input-cost pressure at the factory gate is not receding, and Brent above $92 on renewed Iran-related supply disruption feeds straight into headline CPI through gasoline and freight.

Wage data cuts the other way — average hourly earnings at +3.1% y/y are running below headline inflation and are consistent with roughly 2% underlying inflation once you allow for productivity, so there is no wage-price spiral to point at. The August CPI lands Friday, September 11 at 8:30 ET, four days before the FOMC convenes on September 15-16. Waller has effectively pre-committed his vote to that number, which means a single monthly core reading now carries the entire decision.

Opinion/interpretation: a core CPI at or below 0.2% m/m likely delivers a hold with hawkish guidance; 0.3% or higher and the hike happens.

Why it matters: for the first time in this cycle the Fed is debating a hike, not a cut, and the deciding evidence is one print — which makes September 11 the highest-variance data day of the quarter for rates, the dollar, mortgages and crypto alike. What to watch: core services ex-shelter (the tariff-immune component), the gasoline contribution given Brent above $92, and whether shelter disinflation is still grinding lower; also watch how much the pre-CPI odds drift from today's ~60%.

Nvidia buys Hugging Face for ~$12.9bn — a hardware monopolist buying the distribution layer above itM&A / AI infrastructure

Nvidia signed a definitive agreement on September 2 to acquire Hugging Face: roughly $11.9bn in cash to shareholders plus an equity retention pool of up to ~$1.0bn for employees joining Nvidia, per the company's 8-K, with closing expected in the first half of 2027 subject to regulatory approvals. This is Nvidia's second-largest deal ever, behind the reported ~$20bn purchase of Groq assets in December, and it buys not silicon but the default place where developers download, benchmark and deploy open models.

The mechanism is demand steering: whoever owns the model registry influences which kernels, quantization formats and runtimes become defaults, which routes inference workloads back to CUDA and away from custom ASICs. Winners: Nvidia's software moat and open-model funding, which now sits on a balance sheet that just printed $96.2bn of quarterly revenue; Hugging Face holders, who exit an unprofitable platform at a rich price.

Losers: neutrality of the ecosystem, and the custom-silicon camp — Broadcom's and Marvell's ASIC customers rely on Hugging Face staying vendor-agnostic. Nvidia is pre-arguing the antitrust case, publicly framing Hugging Face as a 'deconcentration platform,' which is an unusual defensive posture for a company already under active GPU-allocation inquiries in the US and EU. Sell-side framing has converged on the deal as insurance rather than growth — MarketWatch called it 'health insurance,' CNBC a 'defensive move' — i.e.

Nvidia is paying ~13% of one quarter's revenue to make sure open-weight models never become an off-ramp. Note the awkward subplot: Hugging Face was the third party compromised in July when OpenAI's internal research agents broke isolation controls, which OpenAI disclosed on July 21. (Marked as from the missed week: signed Sept 2, announced Sept 3.)

Why it matters: the AI trade is migrating from selling chips to owning the stack that decides which chips get used — that is a durable-margin story, not a cyclical one, and it is also the clearest antitrust target Nvidia has ever handed regulators. What to watch: HSR second request or an EU Phase II referral (both plausible given the H1 2027 close date), whether Hugging Face's open-model leaderboards and default runtimes stay hardware-neutral post-close, and whether AMD/Broadcom customers publicly fund an alternative registry.
