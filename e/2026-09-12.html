🧭 The throughline

Markets enter the September FOMC in a supply-shock regime, not a demand one: core CPI has fallen to 2.4%, the lowest since March 2021, while headline sticks at 3.4% on a 27% gasoline surge — the direct consequence of a Hormuz closed since March and, this week, the loss of Saudi Arabia's last big bypass. The Fed is therefore hiking into cost-push inflation it cannot reach, and the bond market has noticed: a 30-year at 5.36% and a 10-year near 5% through an expanded buyback is a term-premium and fiscal-credibility message, not a policy-path one. Demand is already answering the tightening the long end has done on its own — mortgages at 7.07%, sentiment at 47.8, one-year expectations at 4.6% — which is the standard prelude to a mistake that only gets priced after the fact. Risk assets have split along that seam: the AI buildout keeps spending (Oracle's capex now exceeds its revenue; Nvidia may anchor a ~$2T Anthropic listing) while the market has stopped paying for it (Adobe beat, raised, hit a billion MAUs, and fell), and bitcoin trades as pure duration. Hold the regime as stagflationary supply shock plus a credibility-constrained long end — the live question Wednesday is not whether they hike, but whether the curve treats the hike as control or as confirmation that the Fed is fighting the wrong variable.

September FOMC flipped from coin-flip to near-lock: hike odds jumped to ~90% after Friday's CPI, with two hikes now fully priced for 2026Fed

The week repriced the Fed decisively. Into Thursday 10 Sept, CME FedWatch had a 25bp hike at the 15–16 September FOMC at roughly 66–71% (up from ~61% the prior session) after the August PPI landed at +0.4% m/m and 5.4% y/y. Friday 11 Sept's CPI finished the job: odds jumped to nearly 90% from ~70% a day earlier, and bond traders moved to fully price two hikes by year-end, per Bloomberg.

That would take the target range from 3.50–3.75% to 3.75–4.00% next week, with EY-Parthenon among the shops formally switching its call from hold to hike on Friday. The trigger chain runs back to Chair Kevin Warsh's Jackson Hole remarks in late August — that better-than-expected summer inflation prints did not prove the underlying trend had improved 'clearly and quickly enough' — which roughly doubled hike odds from under 35% to 66.1% overnight.

Warsh has also downgraded the Fed's own signalling apparatus: he declined to submit his own dot in June, breaking a 14-year precedent, and has called for the dot plot to become 'a relic', so next week's projections carry less information than any September SEP in a decade. Winners: cash, short-duration credit, banks with liability-sensitive deposit books. Losers: leveraged borrowers, rate-sensitive small caps, and the Treasury's own interest bill.

Why it matters: this is the first Fed tightening cycle restart since 2022, and it is happening with core inflation falling — a policy stance driven by expectations risk rather than realised core prices, which raises the odds of a policy mistake in either direction. Watch: the 16 Sept statement wording on 'further firming', whether any governor dissents against a hike, whether an SEP/dot plot is published at all and where the 2026 median sits (June had 9 of 18 projecting at least one hike), and Warsh's press conference on whether one hike is a one-off or the start of a sequence. Forecast/opinion flagged: MarketWatch (11 Sept) noted the Fed historically has not stopped at a single hike and floated three as a live scenario — that is analyst opinion, not guidance.

August CPI split in two: headline stuck at 3.4% on a 27% annual gasoline surge, while core fell to 2.4% — the lowest since March 2021Inflation

BLS released August CPI on Friday 11 Sept. Headline rose 0.4% m/m and held at 3.4% y/y; core rose 0.3% m/m — a tenth above consensus — but decelerated to 2.4% y/y from 2.5%, the softest core reading since March 2021. The entire headline problem is energy: gasoline jumped 3.9% m/m and is up 27.4% y/y, accounting for over a third of the monthly all-items increase, while the broad energy index rose 2.1% m/m and 16.3% y/y.

The disinflationary engine is still running underneath: shelter rose 0.3% m/m and cooled to 3.0% y/y from 3.2%, groceries were flat m/m at 2.2% y/y, and overall food rose just 0.1% m/m. The day before, August PPI showed the same shape — final-demand goods +1.1% m/m against services +0.1%, headline 5.4% y/y (a tenth hot) but core PPI +0.2% m/m, a shade soft. Weekly jobless claims on 10 Sept were 206k versus 205k expected, i.e. no labour-market crack to argue against tightening.

The energy shock is geopolitical, not demand-driven: Brent has been above $100 on the Iran conflict, and Houthi forces seized an island in the Bab al-Mandab strait on 11 Sept, prompting Saudi Arabia to shut a major oil pipeline as a precaution.

Why it matters: the Fed is set to hike into a supply shock while its own preferred core measure is at a five-year low — tightening cannot lower the price of Gulf crude, so the hike is aimed at expectations, not at the actual source of the price pressure. That makes the real cost fall on rate-sensitive domestic demand (housing, autos, capex) rather than on the inflation itself. Watch: the August PCE print later this month (MND flagged the hot PPI goods component as feeding through to it), whether core y/y breaks below 2.4% in September, gasoline's path if Bab al-Mandab shipping stays disrupted, and whether shelter keeps sliding toward 2.5%.

Bessent's expanded buyback backfired: 30-year yield hit 5.36%, a 22-year high, and the 10-year closed the week on the cusp of 5%Rates

The Treasury's first enlarged liquidity-support buyback on 10 Sept was meant to cap long-end borrowing costs and did the opposite. Treasury bought only $5.19bn of 10–20 year paper against a $6bn maximum — after Secretary Scott Bessent had lifted per-operation sizes from $2bn to at least $4bn effective 9 Sept — and the shortfall was read as a signal that even the buyer of last resort could not find the bonds at these levels.

Ten-year yields extended to 4.95% that day and finished Friday 11 Sept around 4.96–4.98%, the highest since 2023 and within a couple of basis points of 5%; the 30-year reached 5.34–5.36%, a level last seen in 2004. The rest of the curve closed Friday at roughly 4.32% (1y), 4.63% (2y), 4.79% (5y) — so 2s10s sits near +35bp and 5s30s near +57bp, a classic bear steepening in which the long end sells off faster than the policy-linked front end.

Strategists attribute it to three stacked supply problems: a deficit tracking above 2025's, inflation persistently above target, and a wave of corporate issuance competing with Treasurys for the same duration buyers. Losers: anyone long duration, banks with AFS/HTM bond books, pension and insurance mark-to-market, and the federal interest bill. Winners: new money into cash and long bonds, and savers repricing at the highest long yields in a generation.

Why it matters: a steepening driven by term premium — not by growth optimism — means the market is charging the US government more for fiscal risk even as the Fed tightens, and it transmits straight into mortgages and corporate borrowing regardless of what the FOMC does. It also means the Fed's hike may not be what sets long rates; supply is. Watch: whether 10s decisively break 5% (a psychological line that would be the first since 2007), the size and take-up of the next buyback operations, the coming quarterly refunding guidance, and whether 30s stabilise or push toward the 5.5% area. Note: the 10-year close is reported between 4.95% and 4.98% across sources.

Losers: first-time buyers, builders' backlogs, refinance volumes, and homeowners locked into low rates who cannot move. Winners: cash buyers, and lenders' servicing portfolios, whose value rises as prepayment speeds collapse.

Why it matters: mortgages are the fastest and most politically visible channel from the 30-year Treasury to voters — the NYT on 12 Sept framed high pump prices, climbing mortgage rates and above-target inflation as the core of Trump's midterm problem. A Fed hike on 16 Sept will not directly set the 30-year mortgage, but it will not help it either while term premium is doing the work. Nvidia in talks to anchor the Anthropic IPO with up to $10B — a deal that would be the largest listing in history (~$100B raised at ~$2T) and hard-wires the chipmaker into its own customer's cap tableAI complex

Reuters reported on 11 September that Nvidia is weighing an investment of up to $10 billion as an anchor investor in Anthropic's planned IPO. Bankers have told prospective investors Anthropic could raise upwards of $100 billion at a valuation near $2 trillion, which would eclipse every IPO ever done — SpaceX set the current record in June at $85.7 billion raised on a $1.77 trillion valuation.

Anthropic filed confidentially in June and is expected to make its prospectus public after Labor Day, targeting a listing before the November midterms; its last private mark was a $965 billion post-money Series H in May 2026 that raised $65 billion, so the IPO would ask public markets to roughly double the private valuation in months. For Nvidia this extends an already extraordinary balance-sheet campaign: its equity investments were valued at $99 billion as of 26 July, up from about $7 billion a year earlier, with over $40 billion committed in 2026; it agreed to buy Hugging Face for about $12.93 billion in early September, and it has committed up to $100 billion to OpenAI tied to a 10-gigawatt deployment.

The mechanism is circular by construction — Nvidia funds the buyers of Nvidia chips — which is precisely the structure bears point to when they argue AI demand is partly manufactured. A wildcard landed on Saturday 12 September: Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei published an essay calling for the industry to 'pace the frontier' and slow capability advances, days after an Anthropic researcher quit publicly over safety concerns; Elon Musk and other lab leaders voiced agreement.

Nothing in the report is signed — Reuters' sources said talks are ongoing and could change, Anthropic declined to comment and Nvidia had not responded.

Why it matters: a $100B raise would drain an enormous amount of equity capital out of the market in one transaction and would give public investors their first liquid, direct mark on a frontier lab — a price discovery event for the entire private AI complex, including the SPV and secondary market built on those valuations. Nvidia anchoring it converts a supplier relationship into an equity one and makes the circularity critique concrete and disclosable. Watch: the public S-1 (revenue, gross margin on inference, compute commitments and related-party disclosure of Nvidia's stake), whether the deal prices before the midterms, and whether Amodei's 'slow down' framing shows up as a risk factor in the prospectus — a lab telling regulators to pace capability growth while asking for a $2T valuation is an unusual pairing. Forecast, not fact: any material slip past the midterms would be read by the market as demand-side weakness, not scheduling.
