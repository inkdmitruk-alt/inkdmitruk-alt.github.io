A 25bp hike to 3.75–4.00% is ~90% priced for Wednesday — the real trade is the dot plot, not the decisionWhat is priced

After Friday's August CPI printed 3.4% y/y (vs 3.3% expected) with core at 2.4% but a hot 0.3% m/m, CME FedWatch odds of a hike jumped to roughly 90% from 70% the day before, and Goldman Sachs became the last major bank to retract its no-hike call late Friday. A quarter-point move takes the target range from 3.50–3.75% to 3.75–4.00% against an effective funds rate of 3.63%. Because the decision itself is close to fully discounted, the market-moving content sits in the Summary of Economic Projections: the June dots already had 9 of 18 participants penciling at least one 2026 hike, and three members dissented in July demanding higher rates, so the committee is not short of hawks.

KPMG's Diane Swonk sees three hikes by early 2027, pointing to super-core services running +0.5% m/m and 3% y/y with wages at 3.1%. The 2-year note at 4.63% is a full 100bp above EFFR — arithmetic that embeds far more than one move. Winners if the dots go hawkish: cash and T-bill holders, bank net interest margins on floating assets, short-duration funds. Losers: anyone long duration, leveraged-loan borrowers, and the 2027 earnings estimates built on a lower discount rate.

Why it matters: the hike is not the news — the path is. With 100bp of tightening already in the 2-year, a dot plot showing only one more move would be dovish relative to what is in the price, and a plot showing two or three would force another leg of repricing across every rate-sensitive asset. What to watch Wednesday: the number of 2027 dots, the longer-run neutral estimate, how many dissents appear, and whether the statement keeps or drops supply-shock language. (The three-hike path is Swonk's forecast, not an established fact.)

Warsh has signalled he will hike into a supply shock — and the Treasury Secretary is publicly arguing he shouldn'tWarsh vs Bessent

The repricing began at Jackson Hole, where Fed Chair Kevin Warsh conceded the summer inflation readings beat expectations but said they "do not tell me that underlying trends have meaningfully improved," adding that the committee must be confident inflation is moving to target "clearly and at sufficient speed." Hike odds went from roughly 35% before the speech to 64% immediately after, then to ~90% post-CPI.

The objection is that this is the wrong instrument: gasoline was up 27.4% y/y and 3.9% m/m and supplied more than a third of August's monthly CPI increase, with diesel at a record $6.06/gallon on Friday — all Iran-war supply effects. Wellington-Altus strategist James Thorne put it bluntly: rate hikes cannot produce oil, expand refining capacity or repair disrupted supply routes.

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has made the same argument from inside the administration, saying you traditionally don't raise into a supply shock absent second- or third-order effects, and that core inflation has stayed restrained. That is an unusual open split between the fiscal and monetary arms, and it is why analysts flagged the Fed as being "at odds" with Treasury. Who wins from hiking anyway: the Fed's inflation-expectations anchor and the dollar's long-run credibility. Who loses: cyclical borrowers and any sector where the price pressure is a barrel of crude, not excess demand.

Why it matters: if the Fed tightens into an energy shock it is choosing to defend expectations at the cost of demand — a deliberately recessionary trade-off, and a fight with a Treasury that is simultaneously trying to hold long yields down. What to watch: whether Warsh's press conference explicitly names supply shocks as a reason to look through the headline, whether any governor dissents dovishly, and whether Bessent responds publicly after the decision. (Thorne's and Bessent's positions are opinion/analysis, not consensus.)

The curve is bear-steepening into a hike: 30-year at 5.36%, 10-year at 4.96% after an 18bp week — the opposite of what tightening normally doesThe curve

Friday 11 September closed with the 2-year at 4.63%, the 10-year at 4.96% after climbing 18bp on the week to its highest since 2023, and the 30-year at 5.36%. That leaves 10s2s at roughly +35bp and 10s3m around +96bp — implying a 3-month bill near 4.0%, some 37bp above the 3.63% effective funds rate, so the front end has already banked a hike and change (my arithmetic from the quoted spreads).

A textbook hike flattens the curve because the front end rises and the long end falls on lower future inflation; this curve is doing the reverse, which says the bond market does not believe 25bp fixes an oil-driven price level or the fiscal arithmetic behind it. The structural tell is the 20s30s spread, which closed inverted at -1bp on 4 September (30-year 5.24% vs 20-year 5.25%) and has spent 44% of 2026 with the long bond below the 20-year — a shape that cannot absorb tightening without the 30-year rising.

Treasury is fighting this directly: effective 9 September it at least doubled long-end liquidity-support buybacks in the 10–20y and 20–30y buckets, from a $2bn maximum to at least $4bn per operation, running through the 4 November refunding. That is the fiscal arm buying duration while the monetary arm prepares to tighten. Winners: steepener trades, banks that can fund short and lend long, annuity and pension buyers finally getting 5%+ at the long end. Losers: long-duration bond funds, anyone marking a 60/40 book, and the Treasury's own interest bill.

Why it matters: the long end, not the funds rate, sets mortgages, corporate financing and the discount rate on equities — so a hike that steepens rather than flattens tightens the real economy twice. What to watch: whether the 10-year takes out 5.00% this week, whether 20s30s re-steepens past +8bp (which would signal the long end is done repricing), and the size and take-up of the next buyback operations. Forecast, not fact: one published call sees the 30-year at 5.50% by the 27–28 October FOMC, with 5.75% high and 5.00% low cases.

Hike odds went from ~40% to ~90% and the dollar fell: DXY at 99.09 is down 0.92% on the monthThe dollar

The dollar index finished 11 September at 99.0932, up a trivial 0.05% on the day, consolidating around 99.00 into the CPI print — and down 0.92% over the past month even as the probability of a September hike roughly doubled. It is still up 1.58% year-on-year, so this is a stall rather than a rout, but the direction is wrong: hawkish repricing of 50 percentage points of hike probability should have produced a visible dollar rally.

Two mechanisms explain the miss. First, hiking into an energy shock raises nominal rates without raising expected real rates by as much, because the shock is simultaneously lifting expected inflation and cutting expected growth — the classic stagflationary configuration in which currencies do not reward their central banks. Second, the long-end selloff is being read as fiscal and term-premium risk rather than as tighter policy, and a rising 30-year driven by supply concerns is a currency negative, not a positive.

The cross-check is the yen, which has reached its strongest against the US and Australian dollars in six months. Winners: US exporters and multinationals translating foreign earnings, gold, dollar-funded emerging-market borrowers. Losers: US importers facing tariff costs on top of a softer dollar, and anyone holding the standard long-dollar-into-hikes position.

Why it matters: a dollar that will not rally on a near-certain hike is the market telling you it doubts the policy will restore real yields — and that removes the one channel through which the Fed could mechanically damp imported energy prices. What to watch: the DXY reaction in the 30 minutes after Wednesday's statement (a fall on a hawkish dot plot would be a significant credibility signal), USD/JPY, and whether gold keeps bid through the decision. The stagflation reading is analysis, not an official view.

The transmission runs on two separate clocks. The fast one is prime-linked: prime sits at 6.75% (funds-rate upper bound plus 300bp) and mechanically becomes 7.00% the moment the Fed moves, which repricess HELOCs (national average 7.25%) and variable-rate cards almost immediately, since card APRs are prime plus an issuer margin typically running 12–13 points. Average card rates are already around 23.89% (Q2 2026) with new-card offers at 23.82% in September, so a hike lands on revolving balances right as holiday spending starts — and it lands on a consumer that is already stressed, with auto-loan balances 90+ days delinquent at roughly 5.6% in Q1 2026, above the prior peak, and TransUnion forecasting a fifth straight year of rising 60+ DPD auto delinquency at 1.54%.

The slow clock is mortgages, which track the 10-year rather than the funds rate: Freddie Mac's Thursday 10 September survey put the 30-year fixed at 6.76% (from 6.71% the prior week, 6.35% a year ago) and the 15-year at 6.09% (from 6.04%, 5.50% a year ago), but Friday's daily boards had already moved — Mortgage News Daily at 7.12%, Optimal Blue at 6.81% on a fresh one-year high, with lenders repricing ahead of CPI.

Against a 4.96% 10-year that survey rate is a spread of roughly 180bp, which is thin: it is spread compression, not the Treasury market, keeping the headline number under 7%. Losers: revolvers, subprime auto borrowers, HELOC holders, and anyone who must transact in housing this autumn. Winners: savers rolling T-bills near 4%, banks on the deposit-beta lag, and cash-rich buyers in a frozen housing market.

Why it matters: this hike hits the most fragile borrowers instantly through prime while doing nothing quick for the household inflation they are actually feeling at the pump — and because mortgages key off a long end that is steepening, housing gets tightened by the bond market regardless of what the Fed does with 25bp. What to watch: Thursday's Freddie Mac survey (the first to capture this week's yield move and the decision), whether the 30-year fixed prints above 7% on the weekly series, prime-rate announcements from the major banks within 24 hours of Wednesday, and the next NY Fed household-debt update on card and auto delinquency transitions.
