📊 Macro & Rates 5 items

The Fed raised rates 25 bp to 3.75–4.00% in a 12–0 vote, its first hike since July 2023, and dropped the 'supply shocks' excuse for inflationFed decision

At its Sept 15–16 meeting the FOMC raised the federal funds target range by 25 bp to 3.75%–4.00%. It was the first hike since July 2023 and followed five straight holds. The vote was 12–0 with no dissents; in July the Fed held 9–3, with Beth Hammack, Neel Kashkari and Lorie Logan dissenting in favour of a hike. The implementation note, effective Sept 17, sets interest on reserve balances at 3.90%, the overnight reverse repo rate at 3.75% (cap of $160bn per counterparty per day), the standing repo rate at 4.00% and the primary credit (discount) rate at 4.00%.

Balance-sheet guidance is unchanged: keep reserves ample, buy Treasury bills when appropriate and reinvest agency paydowns into bills. The statement is shorter and firmer than July's. It drops the line that blamed elevated inflation 'in part' on supply shocks such as energy, says the hike 'will support a timelier return' to 2%, and keeps 'The Committee will deliver price stability.' At his press conference Warsh said the test he set at Jackson Hole on Aug 28 had failed: the Fed must be confident underlying inflation is moving to target 'clearly and at sufficient speed', and 'the FOMC decided that this standard has not been satisfied.' He said 'the plain fact is that inflation is too high, and has been for too long,' and that too many CPI and PPI categories are still rising faster than 3% over 6 and 12 months.

The data behind the move: August CPI at 3.4% y/y (+0.4% m/m, core +0.3% vs 0.2% expected), August payrolls up 162K, and August retail sales up 1.2% (reported today). Warsh said three things changed since July (a stronger economy and job market, sticky inflation, and geopolitics), which made for 'a firm unanimous decision'. He refused to discuss President Trump, who has demanded the world's lowest rates, saying 'independence is a two-way street' and the Fed must 'stay in our lane.'

Why it matters: dropping the supply-shock wording means the Fed will no longer wait out energy-driven inflation from the Iran war. A unanimous hike under a chair Trump picked expecting cuts is a strong credibility signal. The losers are borrowers and a White House that wanted easier money before the midterms; the winners are savers and the bond market's confidence in the Fed. Watch: the White House reaction (Council of Economic Advisers chair Christopher Phelan called a hike a 'mistake' on Tuesday), and whether July's three hawkish dissenters push for faster moves.

The risk assessments explain the hike: 17 of 18 officials still see upside risks to PCE inflation, while none now sees upside risk to unemployment (7 did in June) and none sees downside risk to growth (5 did in June). My calculation: with the rate midpoint at 3.875% and July core PCE at 3.3% y/y, the real policy rate is only about 0.6%. That fits FXStreet's summary of Warsh saying colleagues are 'hard-pressed' to call financial conditions restrictive and that the Fed removed 'a dose of accommodation.'

Why it matters: the Fed sees demand-driven inflation in a strong economy, not a supply shock it can wait out. That lifts the expected policy path for 2027–28 by about 50 bp vs June and removes the 2027 cut, which keeps front-end yields and floating-rate borrowing costs high. Households: the prime rate rises to about 7% and card rates follow, but mortgages track the ~5% 10-year yield rather than the Fed; savers gain only if they switch accountsHouseholds

Short-term floating-rate debt reprices first. The bank prime rate is normally the fed funds upper limit plus 3 points, which implies 7.00%, up from 6.75% (my calculation). Home equity lines (HELOCs) tied to prime adjust almost at once, while most adjustable-rate mortgages reset once a year. Credit card rates (APRs) should rise about 25 bp within a couple of billing cycles. LendingTree's Matt Schulz puts that at a dollar or two a month for most cardholders, while WalletHub estimates about $2bn in extra interest over 12 months nationwide.

The average rate on new card offers was already 23.82% as of Sept 11 (LendingTree), and card balances were $1.26tn in Q2, near the $1.28tn record (New York Fed). Fixed mortgage rates follow the 10-year yield, not the Fed's rate. Mortgage News Daily's 30-year fixed was 7.19%, up about 38 bp since Warsh's Aug 28 Jackson Hole speech and more than 1 point above a year ago. Freddie Mac's weekly survey showed 6.76% last week, a 14-month high.

TransUnion estimates every 25 bp adds about $65 a month on the average new mortgage ($389,367 at 6.78%). Existing owners are mostly protected: nearly half of outstanding mortgages carry rates of 4% or less (National Association of Realtors), though existing-home sales fell for a third straight month in August. Car loans average 7% for new and 10.6% for used vehicles (Edmunds), with the average new car at $50,089 (Kelley Blue Book).

Savers win on paper, but banks pass rates through slowly: banks now earn 3.90% on reserves at the Fed, while the average 1-year CD paid only 1.71% in August (FDIC data). The gains go mainly to people who move cash into high-yield online accounts, CDs or money-market funds. Moody's economist Mark Zandi says older, wealthier households with locked-in mortgages and savings come out ahead, while people carrying credit card debt, first-time buyers and lower-income families lose.

Moody's estimates the Iran war has cost the average household about $1,760 as of Sept 11, including $425 from higher interest rates and $930 from energy.

Why it matters: with the 10-year at about 5%, long-term yields are tightening credit more than the 25 bp hike itself, and mortgage and auto costs were rising before the Fed moved. Stacked with fuel prices, this squeezes low-income households hardest just before the midterms. Watch: Freddie Mac's weekly rate on Thursday (Sept 17), banks' prime-rate announcements, and whether online-bank savings rates rise faster than big banks' deposit rates.

Why it matters: a real energy truce is one of the few near-term ways to bring diesel prices down. Losers today: truckers, railroads and farmers during harvest. What to watch: whether Moscow actually sends the facility lists; a date for a Trump-Zelensky meeting; national diesel crossing $6.50; Leningrad's fuel limits through Oct. 1; the roughly one-month repair at Syzran.

The line runs about 750 miles from Abqaiq on the Gulf to Yanbu on the Red Sea, has a design capacity of about 7 mb/d, and was carrying roughly 4 mb/d, about 4% of world supply (Bloomberg via OilPrice). Why it matters: Wednesday's drop is effectively a bet that Wright's timeline is right. If the outage lasts weeks, around 4 mb/d stays off the Red Sea route while Hormuz flows remain below prewar levels. That feeds straight into the inflation the Fed hiked against today, with the 10-year near 4.98%. What to watch: any Saudi or Aramco damage statement and restart date; the EIA weekly stocks report (DTN consensus: -1.3 mb crude for the week to 11.09); tanker incidents in Hormuz; Houthi moves at Bab al-Mandab.
