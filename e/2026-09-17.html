Dot plot: one more hike in 2026, no change in 2027, first cut only in 2028. Markets price even more tighteningFed

The FOMC voted 12–0 on Sept. 16 to raise the federal funds rate by 25 bp to 3.75%–4.00%, its first hike since July 2023. The median projection for end-2026 rose to 4.1% from 3.8% in June, which means one more quarter-point hike. 16 of 18 participants expect another increase, four of them see two more, and Chair Warsh did not submit a projection. The 2027 median is also 4.1% (June: 3.6%), so a full year with no change; the committee is split 8 for a further hike, 6 for holding and 4 for cuts.

The first cut in the median appears only in 2028 (3.9%, versus 3.4% in June), and the long-run neutral rate estimate rose to 3.2% from 3.1%. Officials raised 2026 PCE inflation to 3.7% (core 3.4%), lifted GDP growth to 2.3% and lowered the unemployment forecast to 4.1% from 4.3%. They do not see inflation back at 2% until 2029. Warsh said: "Inflation is too high and has been for too long." He also described the hike as removing some accommodation and said he could not call financial conditions restrictive.

In other words, the Fed does not think 3.75%–4.00% is tight yet. Markets are more hawkish than the Fed: per Reuters, investors price more than one more hike this year and about three more by end-2027. Who wins and loses: savers and money-market funds gain, while floating-rate borrowers and the federal interest bill lose. President Trump answered by calling for rates of 1% or lower.

Why it matters: the message changed from 'cuts are delayed' to 'rates stay higher for two years'. That puts a floor under 2- and 5-year yields and makes refinancing plans for 2026–27 harder. The unanimous vote also weakens the argument that political pressure will force quick cuts. Watch: whether the Fed hikes or skips at the October meeting days before the midterms, any escalation of Trump's pressure on the FOMC, and incoming CPI and energy prices, which GSAM calls the deciding factor for December (forecast).

NBU hikes to 16%: emerging markets hit by the oil shock must tighten, while high-rate Brazil still cutsEM / Ukraine

The National Bank of Ukraine raised its key rate by 50 bp to 16% on Sept. 17. It was the second hike in a row after July 30 (15% to 15.5%), and most bankers polled by Interfax-Ukraine expected it. August inflation rose to 8.1% y/y from 7.7% in July and 7.2% in June, above the NBU's July forecast. The drivers were fuel prices linked to the Middle East war and regulated tariffs pushed up by Russian strikes on infrastructure.

Inflation-adjusted, the rate is now about 7.9 percentage points (our own calculation). The NBU's stated aim is to keep hryvnia assets attractive and the currency market stable while bringing inflation back toward its 5% target; it expects inflation to slow only in 2027. It also said official financing in July–August was lower than expected, which forced tighter government spending and reduced international reserves.

In effect, the rate is doing part of the job that delayed foreign aid is not doing. The NBU said the hike will not hold back lending, since demand for hryvnia deposits and domestic government bonds stayed strong after July. It is ready to tighten further if risks grow, but would consider easing if a worse security situation weakens demand and the labor market. Globally the picture is mixed: the Fed and the ECB (its second hike this year, last week) are tightening, the BoE held and the BoJ is expected to hike Friday.

Brazil's central bank, by contrast, cut its Selic rate 25 bp to 13.75% on Sept. 16, its fifth cut in a row, because its high inflation-adjusted rate still attracts carry-trade investors. In Ukraine, hryvnia depositors and holders of domestic bonds gain, while borrowers and the budget's interest bill lose.

Why it matters: a firmer Fed and a dollar index back above 100 make easing harder for any emerging-market central bank whose inflation runs through fuel prices and the exchange rate. Ukraine's hike shows that countries importing the oil shock pay twice, through prices and through higher local rates. Watch: demand for hryvnia deposits and domestic government bonds, reserve figures depending on whether foreign financing arrives, and whether oil's retreat on extra Saudi supply lasts, since fuel is the NBU's main inflation channel.

How the hike reaches borrowers: 30-year mortgage at 7.24% (MND), prime rate at 7%, builders cut prices and Lennar lowers targetsHousing & credit

Mortgage News Daily's 30-year fixed index rose to 7.24% on Sept. 16, a 52-week high (the low was 5.99%) and about 223 bp above that day's 5.01% 10-year close (our own calculation). Freddie Mac's weekly survey, published at noon ET Thursday, showed 6.95%, up from 6.76% a week earlier and 6.26% a year ago; the 15-year was 6.26%. Short-term rates pass through immediately: JPMorgan, KeyCorp and BNY raised their prime rate to 7% from 6.75%, effective Thursday.

That resets most variable credit-card rates within one or two billing cycles on $1.26 trillion of card balances (New York Fed, Q2). Builders are absorbing the hit through price. The NAHB/Wells Fargo builder index fell 3 points to 32 in September, its lowest since September 2025. 38% of builders cut prices (35% in August), the average cut stayed at 6% for a sixth month, and 66% used sales incentives, the most since December.

Lennar's Sept. 16 results confirm the trend: Q3 earnings of $1.19 per share versus $1.28 expected, new orders down 9% to 20,879, an average price of $372,000 (vs $383,000) with about 12% in incentives, and a home gross margin of 15.8% (17.5% a year ago). It cut its 2026 delivery target to 80,000–81,000 homes from 82,000–83,000; the stock closed down 2.1% at $78.36 and fell 2.6% more after hours.

In August, single-family starts jumped 7.6% to 918,000, but single-family permits fell 1.8% to 878,000 and total starts fell 2.6% to 1.275 million, so fewer homes are in the pipeline. Corporate credit has so far shrugged off the hike: the high-yield spread over Treasuries narrowed to 2.70% on Fed day from 2.76%, the investment-grade spread to 0.78% from 0.80%, and CoreWeave launched a $3 billion convertible bond plus a share-sale program worth about $2.92 billion.

Our analysis: large builders that can pay for rate buydowns gain market share, while first-time buyers, smaller builders and people carrying card balances bear the cost.

Why it matters: the hike moves card and home-equity rates through the prime rate right away, but mortgages follow the 10-year yield. Thursday's dip toward 4.95% could trim daily mortgage quotes slightly (forecast). Meanwhile tight credit spreads mean companies still borrow cheaply compared with households, a split that tends to hit housing and consumer spending first. Watch: Mortgage News Daily's Sept. 17 reading (back under ~7.2% would signal relief), Friday's BoJ, the next home-sales data and NAHB survey, and whether high-yield spreads stay near 270 bp if the 10-year returns to 5%.

Congress sends Trump the Graham Russia–Iran sanctions bill (House 262–159). It lets him put tariffs of up to 100% on the biggest buyers of Russian oil and gas, which puts India and China squarely in range.Sanctions / Trade

The House passed the Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act of 2026 by 262–159 on Wednesday, Sept 16, its last day in session before the midterm recess. Per CBS and ABC, 58 Democrats voted yes and 7 Republicans voted no. The Senate had already passed it 86–11 in August. The bill allows, but does not require, tariffs of up to 100% on the top five buyers of Russian crude or gas and on countries that help Russia dodge sanctions.

It also gives the president waiver authority (CBS), a 180-day review period, and an exemption for countries that get less than 15% of their gas from Russia and are cutting imports (Kyiv Independent, CBS). It sanctions Putin, senior officials, the central bank, Sberbank, Gazprombank and the shadow fleet, and stops Iran energy and weapons sanctions authority from lapsing (ABC). A White House official told CNBC that Trump intends to sign.

The exposure is concentrated. As of end-August, China bought about half of Russia's crude exports and India 37% (CREA, via CNBC). Russian barrels were more than 50% of India's crude purchases in June–July and over 40% in August (Kpler). Kpler's Ivan Ryabov told CNBC that replacing 3.5m bpd of seaborne Russian supply, plus about 600k bpd piped to China, would be "extremely challenging." The US raised its tariff on India to 50% last August over Russian oil and cut it to 18% in February.

India's foreign ministry said the country "remains firmly committed to ensuring energy security for its 1.4 billion people" (CNBC). Hungary's parliamentary foreign-affairs chair Márton Hajdu is pressing Washington for an exemption (Ukrainska Pravda). Democrats objected to the tariff power, not the sanctions. Hakeem Jeffries said the bill could give Trump "unfettered authority to unleash tariffs on the American people" (Guardian), and Rep. Don Beyer warned of tariffs "with no guardrails or oversight, and no expiration" (NPR).

Why it matters: until now, tariffs on buyers of Russian oil were a threat Trump could make or drop. This bill writes that power into law with no expiry. If it is used, the likely losers are Indian refiners and Indian exporters to the US, who are still negotiating a trade deal; suppliers of non-Russian crude would gain (this is our analysis). Forecasts, all opinion: Hoover's Ronak Desai expects Trump to sign and "hold its tariff authority in reserve" as leverage. ISEAS's Stephen Olson says Washington is unlikely to "upset the apple cart" before next week's Trump–Xi summit. PIIE's Martin Chorzempa says China "would certainly retaliate" if hit. We found no market move tied to the bill; oil's drop on Thursday came from Saudi supply news. What to watch: the signing and any waivers or exemption list, whether India asks for an exemption (DGA's Atman Trivedi expects it to "quietly seek confirmation"), and what comes out of the Trump–Xi summit.

Trump threatens the EU with "very serious tariffs" over its offer to make Canada an "associate member." Carney calls it "a positive process" and says no one will dictate Canada's partners.Trade / EU–Canada

In her annual State of the EU speech in Strasbourg on Wednesday, Ursula von der Leyen offered to open "the door for Canada to being the first associate member" of the EU. That category does not exist in EU treaties, so member states would have to create it and ratify it (CNBC). Trump landed in North Carolina the same day and told reporters the idea was "laughable" and that "Canada has been a terrible trade partner." He added: "if I think it's at all a hostile act, I will put very serious tariffs or stop trading with Europe on many things" (CNBC, Guardian).

None of those reports cite a tariff rate, product list or start date. Two things are at stake. Last year's US–EU framework caps tariffs on most EU exports at 15% (CNBC). Separately, the US already charges 50% on Canadian goods, plans to ban Canadian dairy, alcohol and auto imports later this month, and Ottawa is retaliating dollar for dollar (CNBC). Carney spoke to the European Parliament on Thursday without naming Trump: "Economic integration is now being weaponized — tariffs being used to exert pressure." He then told reporters, "This alliance is a positive process; that's my response to the American president," added that no one will "dictate … with whom we can strike agreements," and promised a vote in Canada's Parliament (CNBC).

Europe is not united on the name. France's Europe minister Benjamin Haddad said it is "not up to the United States" to choose Europe's direction, while Chancellor Merz's spokesperson asked for the "associate membership" label to be "reconsidered" (Guardian). The practical links are thinner than the rhetoric. CETA, the 2017 EU–Canada trade deal that removed about 99% of tariff lines, is still not ratified by 10 member states, including France, Italy and Poland (Guardian). Canada–EU trade was about C$178.6bn in 2025 (TIME).

Why it matters: the Canada dispute now threatens the 15% cap, which has been the main source of calm in US–EU trade. The companies at risk are EU exporters currently protected by that cap. Canada is speeding up its shift toward Europe, and on Wednesday it also applied to join the UK-led Joint Expeditionary Force (Guardian). CFR's James Lindsay (opinion) says that even if Washington and Ottawa find an off-ramp, "Canada will continue to reduce its vulnerability to U.S. economic pressure." The threat did not visibly move US stocks, which were up about 1.1% intraday at time of publication on falling oil and yields. What to watch: whether Trump puts the threat in writing, whether EU capitals rename or shrink the offer, and the planned US ban on Canadian dairy, alcohol and autos later this month.

Court orders Google to rewrite its ad-auction rules but lets it keep AdX. Rival ad-tech stocks jump and Alphabet edges up.Antitrust / Ad tech

Judge Leonie Brinkema (E.D. Va.) ruled on remedies on Sept 2, and the full 106-page opinion became public on Sept 16 (AdExchanger, Reuters). She rejected the DOJ's demand that Google sell AdX, its ad exchange, and refused to force Google to open-source the auction logic of DFP, its ad server for publishers. Instead she ordered behavior changes meant to "pry open to competition the ad tech markets that were injured by Google's unlawful conduct" (The Next Web).

Google must end the "first look" and "last look" advantages that let AdX bid ahead of rival exchanges, and drop the Unified Pricing Rules. Publishers using DFP will no longer have to use AdX. AdX must bid into rival ad servers on the same terms it gives DFP, and Google must build "functionally equivalent" links to Prebid, the open-source tool publishers use to collect competing bids.

Google must also give publishers real-time win/loss bid data, publish documentation on how DFP picks winners, and stop AdWords from bidding directly into DFP. These rules apply worldwide (AdExchanger). The remedies last six years (the DOJ asked for 15), start 60 days after the final order is signed, and a joint proposed final judgment is due Oct 2. DOJ Associate Attorney General Stanley Woodward Jr. called it a "significant victory," and Google said it will appeal the underlying liability ruling (Reuters).

Stocks on Thursday, intraday per a market report syndicated on AOL: Magnite +5% to about $25 (Craig-Hallum raised its target to $32), PubMatic +6% to $17.79, The Trade Desk −0.5%, Alphabet +0.8% to about $345.60.

Why it matters: Alphabet avoids a breakup. Advertising is 73% of its revenue and AdX charges publishers a 20% fee (Reuters). But its built-in auction edge now shifts toward independent sell-side platforms (the ad-tech firms that sell ad space for publishers) and toward publishers. That is why Magnite and PubMatic rallied while the buy-side Trade Desk barely moved. The real revenue effect is months away and depends on how strictly the rules are enforced. Open Markets Institute's Barry Lynn (opinion) called the ruling proof the judiciary is "abdicating" its duty. What to watch: the Oct 2 joint final judgment, the 60-day start date, Google's appeal, and whether the DOJ also appeals the refusal to order a divestiture.

House passes the Ratepayer Protection Act 417–3 to shift grid-upgrade costs onto big data centers, but states are only required to consider such rules, not adopt them.Energy policy / AI infrastructure

The House passed the Ratepayer Protection Act 417–3 on Wednesday, Sept 16, under a fast-track procedure. It was sponsored by Reps. Gabe Evans (R-Colo.) and Kathy Castor (D-Fla.), and the only no votes were Democrats Summer Lee, Delia Ramirez and Rashida Tlaib (NBC News). It covers data centers drawing more than 100 MW, which Straight Arrow News says is roughly the demand of 70,000 homes.

State utility commissions would have one year from enactment to start considering rules that make those facilities pay the full cost of grid upgrades. The NYT and States Newsroom both stress that states would not be compelled to adopt them. Evans said "Hardworking families should not have to subsidize the energy demands of data centers" (NBC). Rep. Frank Pallone called the bill "one small step" that "does not go far enough," and Public Citizen's Tyson Slocum called it "a far cry from any sort of comprehensive solution" (Straight Arrow News).

The utility trade group Edison Electric Institute backs the bill, and the National Association of State Energy Officials praised the approach (Straight Arrow News, States Newsroom).
