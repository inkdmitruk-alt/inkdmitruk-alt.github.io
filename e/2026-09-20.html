🧭 The throughline

This is a supply-shock regime, not a demand cycle. Core CPI has already fallen to 2.4% — the lowest since March 2021 — yet headline inflation sits at 3.4% because energy is up 16.3% year over year and diesel just printed a record $6.29 a gallon. The Fed responded on 16 September with its first hike since July 2023, taking the target range to 3.75–4.00% in a unanimous 12-0 vote, which means the central bank is now tightening into a price shock that rate policy cannot fix: raising the funds rate does not reopen the Strait of Hormuz or restart the Saudi East-West pipeline. The market is pricing the consequence rather than the cause — the 10-year yield touched 5.00% while the 30-year fell to 5.327%, a flattening from the long end that reads as 'inflation gets beaten, growth pays for it.' Everything else this week is downstream of that: the Dow fell 1.69% while the Nasdaq rose 0.7%, Russell 2000 lagged again, corporate bitcoin treasuries are sitting exactly at break-even, and the single biggest scheduled catalyst — the Trump-Xi summit on 24 September — is about rare earths and tariff truces, which is to say about supply again.

📊 Macro & Rates 4 items

The Fed hiked 25bp to 3.75–4.00% — its first increase in over three years, and the vote was unanimousCNBC / Charles Schwab / Fortune

On 16 September the FOMC raised the target range for the federal funds rate by 25 basis points to 3.75–4.00%, the first hike since July 2023, on a 12-0 vote. The statement states flatly that inflation remains elevated. The dot plot showed 16 of 18 participants expecting at least one more increase this year, with four penciling in two; median projections put the rate at 4.1–4.4% by end-2026.

The political dimension is hard to miss: the Fed is now chaired by Kevin Warsh, sworn in on 22 May 2026 after Trump pushed out Jerome Powell precisely over his refusal to cut, and Warsh voted with everyone else to tighten, explaining simply that inflation is too high. Equities fell on the Wednesday decision and then recovered the next day, with the S&P 500 up over 1% and the Nasdaq adding roughly 400 points. Futures now price the funds rate near 4.2% by December and about 4.7% by September 2027.

Why it matters: a president installed his own Fed chair and the chair hiked anyway. For now political pressure is not transmitting into policy — but if that pressure shifts from posts to personnel or institutional moves, the long end will start charging a premium for Fed independence, and 30-year yields would rise faster than the short end rather than slower.

Sources: CNBC · Charles Schwab · Fortune

Headline CPI 3.4%, core down to 2.4% — the entire gap is energyBLS / NBC News

The Bureau of Labor Statistics report released 11 September put August CPI at +0.4% on the month and +3.4% year over year. Core CPI, excluding food and energy, slowed to +2.4% from 2.5% — the lowest reading since March 2021. The divergence is entirely a supply story: the energy index is up 16.3% over the year, gasoline 27.4%, fuel oil 52%. Gasoline alone rose 3.9% in August and accounted for more than a third of the monthly increase in the whole index.

Shelter decelerated to 3.0% from 3.2% and groceries to 2.7% from 3.0%. This is the classic energy-shock trap for a central bank: the component it can influence is already close to target, while the component driving the headline is set by tanker routes and pipeline outages.

Why it matters: if core keeps sliding toward 2% while headline stays above 3%, the unanimity of this month's vote will not survive. That is where the first dissents come from — and the first dissent is usually the market's earliest signal that the hiking cycle has a ceiling.

Sources: BLS · NBC News

Payrolls came in at 162,000 against a 53,000 consensus — the labour market gave the Fed no excuseNBC News / BLS / Trading Economics

August payrolls came in at 162,000, more than three times the Dow Jones consensus of 53,000, with the unemployment rate holding at 4.1%. Weekly initial jobless claims printed 196,000 against 207,000 expected. That combination — a hot payroll surprise, stable unemployment and falling claims — removed the last argument for holding rates steady into an energy shock, because it means the economy is absorbing $100 oil without shedding jobs.

The counterweight, and the reason this is not simply a strong economy, is that wage growth continues to run behind inflation, which is why consumer sentiment surveys remain depressed even as the labour data improves. Goldman Sachs has taken to explaining the gap between solid activity data and miserable sentiment as a happiness problem rather than an economic one.

Why it matters: the wage-versus-inflation gap decides whether this energy shock stays a one-off price level shift or turns into a second round through pay demands. Watch average hourly earnings against headline CPI — that spread, not the payroll number, is what the Fed is actually watching.

Why it matters: a 5% ten-year breaks the arithmetic of growth-equity valuation, because the discount rate applied to distant cash flows jumps and the highest-multiple names reprice first. The second channel is housing — new home sales for August and KB Home earnings both land this week, and that is where the rate effect shows up fastest.

⚡ Energy & Commodities 4 items

Brent near $103.85 and WTI near $100.65 — priced by blocked supply, not by demandCNBC / IEA

Brent fell about 0.9% on Friday to $103.83–103.87 a barrel and WTI to $100.30–101.01, but both finished the week up roughly 2%. The level above $100 has nothing to do with demand: the naval blockade of Iran, reinstated in July, has halted new Iranian export loadings from the Persian Gulf, and the shutdown of Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline on 11 September removed a 7 million barrel per day route that was supposed to compensate for tankers stuck in the Gulf.

Saudi output has fallen to its lowest since 1990, the US Strategic Petroleum Reserve is near a record low, and China was building inventory through August. Friday's move down reflected two competing headlines: fresh exchanges of fire between Saudi Arabia and the Houthis on one side, and Riyadh saying East-West could restart within days on the other. The IEA, meanwhile, forecasts global oil demand falling by 2.5 million barrels per day in 2026 — the price is pure supply disruption.

Why it matters: the restart of East-West is the single highest-leverage scheduled event in commodities right now. If it comes back, Brent likely heads into the $90s and the pressure on the Fed eases; if another strike takes it out again, the market will price two more hikes rather than one.

Sources: CNBC · IEA

Diesel at a record $6.29 a gallon, up 68% year over year — the real inflation transmission channelCNBC / US energy data

The most consequential price in this market is not crude but distillate. Diesel reached a record $6.29 a gallon, up 68% from a year ago, having already broken the 2022 all-time high of $5.82 on 11 September. Diesel is not a consumer discretionary item — it is an input cost for trucking, rail, agricultural machinery and construction, which means it propagates into the price of physical goods with a lag of roughly two to four months rather than showing up immediately in CPI.

That lag is precisely why the Fed is acting on forecasts rather than on the current print: the August energy contribution to CPI reflects gasoline, while the diesel shock is still working its way through freight contracts. Refining margins for distillate have widened sharply as the blockade removed heavy sour barrels that yield diesel most efficiently.

Why it matters: watch trucking spot rates and the producer price index for intermediate goods over the next two prints. If they turn up together, the energy shock has moved from the pump into the goods economy, and core CPI's descent toward 2% stalls.

🚢 Trade, Tariffs & Policy 4 items

Trump signed the Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act — a 30-day clock on tariffs of up to 100%The Hill / GV Wire / US News

On 18 September the president signed the Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act of 2026 into law. It targets Russia's energy and defence sectors and the shadow fleet of tankers used to evade existing measures, and it directs the president to impose tariffs of up to 100% on goods from the largest purchasers of Russian energy or on countries helping Russia evade sanctions.

The tariffs must be imposed within 30 days of enactment — roughly by 18 October. The statute names no countries, leaving the designation to the administration; the largest buyers of Russian crude are China and India. It also grants broad authority to waive tariffs or sanctions for national security reasons simply by notifying Congress, which critics call a get-out-of-jail-free card.

The Senate passed it 86-11 in August, the House 262-159 last week. This is the first time in nearly forty years that Congress has handed the executive new tariff authority.

Why it matters: the waiver clause is the whole trade. If the administration uses it broadly, this is a signalling instrument; if it does not, 100% tariffs on Chinese and Indian goods weeks before a midterm election would be a direct hit to US consumer prices during an energy shock. India has already warned about bilateral damage and Moscow says it makes a peace deal harder.

A $14bn Taiwan arms package approved in January but not yet formally notified sits in the background. Sam Altman, Jensen Huang, Cristiano Amon and Tim Cook are expected at the dinner.

Why it matters: rare earths are where geopolitics touches semiconductor, EV and defence production directly. A short extension simply rebuilds a deadline wall for the winter and brings semiconductor volatility back. The guest list suggests chip export licences are on the table — watch for anything about Nvidia's access to the Chinese market.

The CLARITY Act died 49-50 in the Senate — regulators are now writing the rules insteadCNBC / CoinDesk

What killed it was ethics rather than substance: Democrats demanded a legally enforceable bar on the president and senior officials profiting from crypto while writing its rules. Why it matters: rules made by exemption are less durable than rules made by statute — what one SEC grants, the next can withdraw without Congress. That is the path to 24/7 trading of US stocks.
