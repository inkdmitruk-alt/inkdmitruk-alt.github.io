📊 Macro & Rates 4 items

The Fed hiked for the first time since 2023 — and told markets it isn't doneFederal Reserve / CNBC

On Sept 16 the FOMC raised the target range 25bp to 3.75%-4.00% in a unanimous 12-0 vote, the first increase in more than three years. Chair Kevin Warsh framed it as securing a 'timelier return' to the 2% objective — two words that sank all three major indices on the day. The dot plot shows 16 of 19 officials expecting at least one more hike this year; for 2027, eight see another hike, six see a hold and four see cuts.

Fed funds futures put October odds near 49-50% and the probability of at least one more hike by year-end at roughly 87%. Critically, this is a supply-shock tightening: August CPI ran 3.4% year over year with gasoline up 27.4% and fuel oil up 52%, and gasoline alone contributed more than a third of the monthly CPI increase. The Fed is raising rates against an energy shock it cannot drill its way out of. The next meeting is Oct 27-28.

Why it matters: a central bank tightening into an energy-driven price shock destroys demand to offset supply it cannot control. That is the classic recipe for a policy-induced downturn — and the September CPI print on Oct 14 is the swing factor for whether the October hike happens.

Ten-year yields retreat from 19-year highs as the oil premium deflatesTrading Economics / CNBC

The 10-year Treasury yield slipped back below 5% and traded near 4.94%, after shedding almost 10bp in the prior session — a retreat from 19-year highs driven by softer crude. The 30-year sits around 5.34%. The move is almost entirely an inflation-expectations story rather than a growth story: cheaper oil lowers the expected path of headline CPI, which lowers the term premium investors demand.

Equities and crypto both rallied on exactly the same impulse, which is why cross-asset correlation was near one today. The fragility is that nothing structural changed — the Fed is still hiking and refined product supply is still constrained. A single headline reversing the Iran diplomacy story puts 5% back in play within a session.

Why it matters: with the 30-year above 5.3%, every corporate refinancing, mortgage and government interest line reprices upward. Duration risk is now the dominant risk in balanced portfolios, not equity beta.

The Treasury is easing while the Fed is tighteningCNBC / Fortune

As 30-year yields approached a near-20-year high in August, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent doubled the size of the department's long-dated debt buyback operation, cutting effective supply and pushing yields down. Bessent called the operation a 'Treasury Twist' — buying long-term Treasuries funded by short-term issuance. CNBC reported the near-$1 trillion Treasury General Account could be tapped to fund the buybacks.

His stated objective since January has been lowering the 10-year yield to ease borrowing costs for households and businesses. The intervention's effect was short-lived: yields retraced the entire decline the following day. Bessent has drawn criticism for attempting to manage prices in the bond market directly, at the same moment the central bank is deliberately raising them.

Why it matters: this is an open fiscal-versus-monetary conflict. If markets conclude fiscal dominance is emerging, term premium rises rather than falls — defeating the entire operation. Watch the next quarterly refunding announcement.

The bank cited the risk that inflation deviates upward beyond its 2% target, and expects core CPI to accelerate past 2% in the second half of fiscal 2026 because of high oil prices stemming from the Iran war. Why it matters: a yen that weakens on a rate hike means the market doubts the BOJ will keep pace — and JGB yields near 3% make repatriation by Japanese institutions rational, draining a structural bid from US Treasuries and global credit.

🌐 Geopolitics & the Economy 4 items

Trump signs the Graham Act: authority for 100% tariffs on buyers of Russian energyThe Hill / RFE-RL

Trump signed H.R. 5334, the Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act of 2026, on Sept 18. The law authorizes tariffs of up to 100% on goods imported from countries falling within specified categories tied to purchases of Russian oil and natural gas or to circumvention of US sanctions. It targets Russia's energy and defense industries and its shadow fleet of sanctions-evading tankers, and authorizes sanctions on the five largest buyers of Russian energy and the five countries doing most to help Moscow evade energy sanctions.

It also extends the Iran Sanctions Act of 1996 through 2031. The mechanism is secondary rather than primary: the pain is transmitted through Russia's customers, principally India and China. Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov said the additional sanctions would 'certainly complicate' a settlement. Crucially the statute grants authority, not obligation — it is leverage to be traded, not an automatic strike.

Why it matters: applying a 100% tariff to India or China would be an inflationary act inside the US at the exact moment the Fed is fighting 3.4% CPI. Watch whether the lever is quietly shelved around the Xi summit.

Sources: The Hill · RFE/RL

Secondary-tariff authority fuses sanctions and trade into one instrumentRFE-RL / Moscow Times

The Graham Act's authorization of tariffs up to 100% on countries buying Russian energy merges sanctions policy and trade policy. Historically sanctions targeted entities and tariffs targeted goods; this statute lets energy purchasing decisions trigger blanket trade penalties. The five-largest-buyers provision effectively names India and China without naming them. For importers and supply chains, country risk must now be modelled on energy sourcing, not just on direct trade relationships.

The discretionary structure means the threat is continuously live but never certain, which is itself a cost — it raises hedging expense across affected trade lanes.

Why it matters: permanent optionality on 100% tariffs is a standing tax on planning. Companies with India or China exposure now carry an unhedgeable policy risk that did not exist before Sept 18.

A memorandum strips Canadian-origin goods out of US federal procurementFederal Register 91 FR 59979

A presidential memorandum signed Sept 16 and published Sept 21 directs the OMB Director and the US Trade Representative, coordinating with the Federal Acquisition Regulatory Council, to identify Canadian-origin items in the federal civil procurement system and take all lawful steps to make them unavailable for purchase, while notifying agencies of domestic alternatives. The stated cause is Canada's 'Buy Canadian' preferences and provincial procurement restrictions, set against the fact that Canadian firms retain access to US federal procurement covered under the WTO Government Procurement Agreement — over $280 billion annually.

The USTR is to monitor Canadian treatment of US-origin goods and advise on whether access should be restored if policy changes.

Why it matters: this is a memorandum, not an executive order — it creates no enforceable rights and works through FAR amendments. The real impact date is whenever those rule changes publish, not today.

The ECB launches Pontes to defend the euro against dollar stablecoinsCoinDesk / Cointelegraph

The ECB launched Pontes on Monday, enabling banks and eligible financial institutions to settle tokenized-asset transactions in central bank money. Pontes connects distributed-ledger platforms to the Eurosystem's TARGET Services, offering an alternative to both stablecoins and tokenized commercial-bank deposits for wholesale transactions. Services and operating hours will expand gradually, with full implementation expected by 2028.

The ECB's stated motive is explicit: it views adoption of private dollar-backed stablecoins such as Tether's USDT and Circle's USDC as a threat to Europe's monetary autonomy. The competitive mechanism is the settlement asset — whoever controls the settlement leg controls the currency of the transaction. The ECB also announced it will invest in tokenized securities using its own funds via the platform.

Why it matters: a 2028 full-implementation horizon means pressure on USDC and USDT in Europe is a 2027-28 story, not a current-quarter one. But the direction is structural, and it closes the highest-margin wholesale market to US issuers.
