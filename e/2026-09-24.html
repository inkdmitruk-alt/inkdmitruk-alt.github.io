Losers are floating-rate borrowers (credit cards, HELOCs, leveraged loans, small caps). Winners are money-market savers and bank asset yields. 🚢 Trade & the Trump–Xi Summit 5 items

The trade truce was extended only to Jan. 10: a two-month leash instead of the 6–12 months many had expectedUS–China / Trade

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said on Fox News on Wednesday evening (Sept. 23), as Xi landed at Joint Base Andrews, that the Busan truce will now run to Jan. 10, 2027. It had been due to lapse on Nov. 10. His words: 'We have agreed today that we will extend what we call the Busan Agreement' (quoted by MS NOW; CNBC and AP also reported it). The rollover keeps the October-2025 bargain in place.

The U.S. keeps its higher reciprocal tariffs and its port fees on Chinese-built ships suspended; in return Beijing keeps new rare-earth curbs paused, buys U.S. farm goods and acts on fentanyl precursors (Bloomberg via Illinois Farm Policy News). The rollover does not lower the tariffs already in place. The Congressional Research Service puts the average U.S. tariff on Chinese goods at about 36.5% in July, against China's roughly 31% on U.S. goods, and a 12.5% forced-labor levy (in force since July) and Section 232 duties of up to 50% stay (CNBC).

Bessent sounded lukewarm: 'I don't know whether a bigger deal can be done. I don't know whether we will just roll the current deal' (AP), and 'there's some deliverables that have not been perfect on the Chinese side' (Bloomberg). Beijing has not officially confirmed the new date (NPR). Commerce Ministry spokesman He Yadong said only that the two sides discussed cutting tariffs and extending the arrangements agreed in Kuala Lumpur (CNBC).

Many had expected an extension of six months or longer (CNBC), and Bank of America's base case was one year. CSIS's Scott Kennedy says the two-month term shows the U.S. is 'unsatisfied with China's offers and wants to keep the heat on' (CNBC). The main 'bigger deal' option is a reciprocal tariff cut of about $30 billion on each side for non-critical goods, which Bessent calls '30-by-30' and which would run through the U.S.-China Board of Trade.

The U.S. would sell more farm, energy and medical-device goods; China would sell more consumer and low-tech goods (Bessent and USTR Greer to CNBC, Sept. 20–21). Still unresolved: rare-earth export licensing. The EU Chamber in China says there is still no standard way to apply for a license (CNBC).

Why it matters: the deadline moves from Nov. 10, a week after the midterms, to Jan. 10, after APEC in Shenzhen (November) and the G20 in Miami (December). Tariff and rare-earth risk therefore becomes a rolling two-month negotiation instead of a one-year hedge (analysis). Who gains: importers and magnet-dependent U.S. manufacturers, who avoid a snapback in November. Who loses: companies that need visibility on sourcing for several quarters. Also: Southeast Asian hubs lose if a U.S.-China tariff cut narrows their tariff advantage (Eurasia Group and ISEAS via CNBC). What to watch: any readout or fact sheet after today's bilateral meeting and tonight's East Room state dinner; whether Beijing confirms the Jan. 10 date; whether a 30-by-30 list of goods is published.

Farm-state pressure: China's 10% soybean duty still bites, and White House aides reportedly weigh scaling back the beef-import planAgriculture / Trade politics

Bessent says China has been 'very fulsome' on soybean purchases this year but is 'a little behind schedule' on other farm commitments (Bloomberg via Illinois Farm Policy News). Under the Busan deal, China committed to buy 25 million tonnes of U.S. soybeans a year through 2028 (investingLive). A 10% Chinese import duty still keeps private Chinese buyers out, and USDA reported no U.S. soybean sales to China this week (Investing.com).

Separately, Politico reported on Wednesday that a small group at the White House Domestic Policy Council, which Vince Haley heads, is looking at scaling back Trump's August order. That order lets 300,000 metric tons of beef in at discounted prices without certain tariffs, and the review comes as Republicans worry about the midterms. USTR, which put the plan into effect, disagrees with cutting it back.

On the record, White House spokesman Kush Desai said 'The White House is not considering such action', and a USTR official said there is 'no pending policy change' (Politico). The imports are lean beef trimmings over 90 days, about 2–3% of U.S. consumption. They are meant to bring down record ground-beef prices while the U.S. cattle herd is at a 75-year low; the American Farm Bureau Federation called the plan a 'gut punch' to ranchers (Foreign Policy, Sept. 15).

Iowa's Republican Senate nominee Ashley Hinson and Michigan's Mike Rogers have publicly broken with Trump over costs. Politico also says the White House is preparing a plan on diesel exports, likely with exemptions.

Why it matters: beef and soybeans show the domestic price of trade policy six weeks before the midterms. The beef imports help shoppers at ranchers' expense.
