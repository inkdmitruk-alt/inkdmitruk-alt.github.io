30-year Treasury yield hits 5.44%, highest since 2004Rates

The 30-year yield closed Wednesday at 5.39%, its highest since July 2004, and rose as high as 5.44% (+4 bp) Thursday morning, per Investing.com and Bloomberg via Yahoo Finance. The 10-year traded around 5.10%, its first time at that level in 19 years. The 20-year hit 5.33%, its highest close since June 2007. Every point on the curve except the 2-year now yields above 5%. Columbia Threadneedle's Ed Al-Hussainy said investors want 'much higher compensation' to lock money up for 30 years, a textbook term-premium story.

The drivers are inflation, heavier Treasury borrowing, and oil. The bond buyback program Treasury expanded in mid-August has, per Bloomberg, had little sustained impact.

Why it matters: 5%+ long yields reprice everything from mortgages to equity multiples, and rising interest costs worsen the deficit, which feeds back into higher yields.

Sources: Yahoo/Bloomberg: US 30-year yield hits highest since 2004 · Investing.com: 30-year yield hits 5.39% · CNBC

Hot flash PMIs light the fuseData

S&P Global's flash manufacturing PMI came in at 57.0 against a 53.6 forecast, and services hit 58.7 against 55.8, per Investing.com. Both readings point to strong activity, undercutting the thesis that high rates are slowing the economy enough to cool inflation. After the data, the iShares 20+ Year Treasury ETF (TLT) fell 1.58%. The curve bear-steepened, with long yields rising faster than short ones. That pattern is typical when markets doubt inflation will come down, not just when they expect the Fed to raise rates.

Why it matters: a strong economy with sticky inflation leaves the Fed little room to stop tightening, and bear steepening hurts long-duration assets most.

Sources: Investing.com: Hot PMI data

'Board of Trade' lists: lower tariffs on selected goodsTariffs

Under Trump's 'Board of Trade' initiative, lower tariffs are proposed for specific lists of goods, NBC reported. The US side covers energy, agriculture and possibly medical devices; the Chinese side covers consumer and low-tech goods. Structurally this is a managed-trade swap: Chinese purchase commitments for US commodities in exchange for cheaper Chinese consumer imports. Winners would be US farm exporters (soybeans), LNG exporters and big-box retailers; domestic producers of competing low-tech goods would lose some protection.

Why it matters: cheaper consumer imports would ease goods inflation slightly, a rare disinflationary offset to oil.

Sources: NBC News
