📊 Macro & Rates 4 items

Treasury selloff extends: 10-year at 5.21%, 30-year at 5.52%, both multi-decade highsCNBC / Signature FD

The benchmark 10-year Treasury yield rose more than 4 basis points on Friday to about 5.209%, after touching 5.208-5.22% on Thursday, its highest since June 2007. The 30-year bond climbed more than 5 basis points to 5.516%, a level last seen in 2004, while the 2-year sat near 4.897%. Thursday's jump was the 10-year's biggest one-day rise since April 2025, driven by a hot flash PMI, hawkish Fed commentary, weak demand at a $70 billion 5-year note auction that pushed 5-year yields above 5%, and firm oil.

The Fed raised rates 25 bp to 3.75-4.00% on Sept. 16 in a 12-0 vote, its first hike since 2023, and its median projection shows one more increase in 2026. CME FedWatch shows roughly 66% odds of another hike in October. The move is a bear steepener led by the long end, signalling that term premium, not just policy expectations, is rising.

Why it matters: long yields above 5% raise the discount rate on every long-duration asset, from growth equities to commercial real estate. If August PCE (Sept. 30) runs hot, an October hike becomes near-certain and the 30-year could push further above 5.5%.

Sources: CNBC — Treasury yields rise again to end a volatile week · Signature FD — The Market Brief 9/25/26 · CNBC — Fed rate decision September 2026

Activity is running hot: flash composite PMI 58.4, jobless claims 197K, while consumer sentiment sinks to 48.1S&P Global / University of Michigan

The S&P Global U.S. flash composite PMI rose to 58.4 in September from 56.0, the strongest private-sector expansion since July 2021 and consistent with roughly 5% annualized GDP growth. Services climbed to 58.7 and manufacturing jumped to 57.0 from 53.9, with the survey flagging building price pressures and supply issues. Initial jobless claims fell to 197,000 from 202,000, a historically tight reading.

Yet households feel worse: the University of Michigan's final September sentiment index was 48.1 (vs. 47.5 expected and 51.7 in August), a four-month low and 15% below January. Year-ahead inflation expectations rose to 4.6%, the highest since June, and five-year expectations ticked up to 3.4% from 3.3%. The combination of strong output, tight labor and rising expectations is precisely the mix that keeps the Fed in tightening mode.

Why it matters: a widening gap between hard activity and consumer mood usually precedes a spending slowdown, but the Fed will react to the inflation-expectations rise first. Watch August PCE on Sept. 30; July core PCE was 3.34% y/y.

Treasury's buyback firepower underwhelms: $4.08B repurchased against $10.5B offeredCNBC / U.S. Treasury

On Thursday the Treasury bought back $4.078 billion of 20- and 30-year bonds, well short of the roughly $10.47 billion dealers offered and below the ~$6 billion the market had expected. The operation is part of the long-end 'liquidity support' program that Secretary Bessent doubled on Aug. 19, lifting the maximum per operation from $2 billion to at least $4 billion for Sept. 9 through Nov. 4.

Analysts say the 10- to 30-year sector has faced a buyers' strike since late June, and Treasury's mix of buybacks and verbal reassurance has had limited impact. Reports last month said Treasury could tap its nearly $1 trillion General Account to fund larger purchases. Bessent has publicly insisted the Treasury market is in 'very good shape'. Markets are reading the modest take-up as a signal that Treasury will not act as a de facto yield-curve-control backstop.

Why it matters: if the fiscal authority cannot or will not cap long yields while the Fed is hiking, the burden of adjustment falls on term premium. A larger, TGA-funded buyback would be the next escalation to watch.

Why it matters: the attack vector (a compromised third-party tool) is an industry-wide risk; expect renewed scrutiny of exchange hot-wallet controls and of how much stolen ETH is laundered through bridges and mixers in coming days.

Fed proposes first GENIUS Act rules for bank-supervised stablecoin issuersFederal Reserve / CoinDesk

On Sept. 24 the Federal Reserve Board requested comment on two proposals implementing the GENIUS Act for issuers it supervises. The first would require full backing with permissible reserves such as short-term Treasury bills and other high-quality liquid assets, set standardized capital requirements for credit and operational risks, and establish risk-management standards. It would also set rules for firms safekeeping reserve assets and clarify which stablecoin activities are permissible for Fed-supervised banks.

The second proposal creates a tailored application, appeals and hearings process for banks seeking to issue payment stablecoins. Comments are open for 60 days after Federal Register publication.

Why it matters: T-bill reserve mandates make stablecoin growth a new source of front-end Treasury demand; bank issuance could challenge Circle and Tether's share once the framework is final.
