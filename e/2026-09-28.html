Forecasts: Bank of America says even a softer headline would not materially change October pricing, and it sees private payrolls at only 60k.

🚢 Trade & Policy 5 items

The US ban on Canadian liquor, whey, molasses and big motorcycles took effect at 12:01 a.m. ET on Sept. 29, turning 50% tariffs into import bansTrade / Canada

Trump signed three proclamations on Sept. 8 (Nos. 11061, 11062 and 11063, published in the Federal Register Sept. 14). They use Section 338 of the Tariff Act of 1930 to bar goods that had paid a 50% duty since Aug. 22. Per Global News' product list, that covers most Canadian alcohol (rye/whisky, wine, beer, vodka, rum, vermouth, tequila), whey protein and whey by-products, cane and invert molasses, and motorcycles above 800cc.

The White House gives three reasons: provincial shelf bans on US liquor (every province except Alberta and Saskatchewan still has one, Global News reports), Saskatchewan's extra 50% levy on US alcohol from Sept. 8, and Canada's quota rules on US cheese and its auto tariff scheme. The proclamations say Canada "reneged" on an Aug. 21 commitment. The damage is concentrated rather than economy-wide.

Canada exported $1.36bn of alcoholic drinks in 2023, 90% of them to the US and two-thirds spirits, and 93% of exported spirits went south last year (Global News). Whey is small: about $73m of US imports last year, nearly half from Canada. Canada shipped only 5,092 motorcycles in 2025 (both from CBC via Yahoo). In the Oval Office on Monday, Trump said Canadians feel entitled and added "There's nothing they have that we need," Global News reported.

USTR Jamieson Greer told Global News the president is comfortable with the paused talks and sees no urgency. Greer also noted the US keeps getting the Canadian oil, gas and potash it needs. Trade Minister Dominic LeBlanc's office said protecting Canadian workers and firms comes first. For context, US 50% tariffs from August cover about C$28bn of goods, and Canada's counter-tariffs on about C$20bn of US imports have applied since Sept. 8 (Global News).

Losers: Canadian distillers and craft brewers (Collective Arts told CBC the 50% duty already made US sales extremely difficult), whey-based nutrition firms such as Windsor's BioSteel, and US importers and bars. The legal detail matters too: goods that arrived but were not yet entered before Sept. 29 still pay 50%. A severability clause brings the 50% duty back automatically if a court strikes the ban down.

Why it matters: a ban removes the number that negotiators could otherwise bargain down. Trade lawyer Barry Appleton told CBC that bans are far harder to unwind than tariffs. The automatic return to a 50% duty shows the White House expects court challenges. What to watch: CBP guidance and any corrections to the product annex in the Federal Register; whether Ontario, Quebec or BC add more curbs on US liquor or procurement; any restart of Greer–LeBlanc talks; and holiday-season shelf prices for Canadian whisky in US stores (our forecast).

After the summit, the US and China agreed '30-for-30' tariff cuts on $60bn of goods and confirmed the truce to Jan. 10, 2027: small sums, but a firm deadlineTrade / US–China

The White House released the lists on Sunday and China's commerce ministry (MOFCOM) confirmed them on Monday. Under a new US–China 'Board of Trade', each side will cut tariffs on $30bn of the other's goods, $60bn in total, with over 90% of product lines going to most-favoured-nation rates (AP). China's list has 1,619 US items. They include farm goods such as poultry, dairy, eggs and peanuts, plus timber, coal, medical equipment and personal-care products.

The US list has just 77 Chinese categories, mostly consumer goods: microwave ovens, tableware, fireworks, wooden Christmas ornaments and soccer balls (AP, Al Jazeera). Chips, EVs and batteries were left out (AP). On Sept. 28 MOFCOM also formally confirmed that the Busan truce is extended by two months, from Nov. 10 to Jan. 10, 2027 (China Briefing). That keeps China's October-2025 rare-earth export controls and the mutual port fees suspended.

Washington says China will import at least 10 million tonnes of US coal in 2027–28. MOFCOM only said the cuts would be 'conducive' to more coal imports. For scale: China's $30bn covers roughly 30% of US exports to China (Al Jazeera). But two-way trade in 2025 was about $470–495bn (China Briefing, Al Jazeera). US exports to China were about $68bn in January–July, while China shipped about $270bn to the US in January–August.

China's global surplus was about $800bn by August (AP). Hinrich Foundation's Deborah Elms told Al Jazeera the lists barely move overall trade. Natixis' Gary Ng told AP the tilt toward consumer goods could slightly ease US inflation. Winners: US farm exporters and US importers of holiday-season goods. Separately, the Guardian reported that the US ambassador said Trump asked Xi whether China would buy US weapons. Trump denied discussing arms sales but said Beijing would probably like to buy them (The Hill).

Why it matters: the cuts are symbolic in size, but they create a standing channel for step-by-step tariff relief. The real risk date is the Jan. 10, 2027 truce deadline, which rare-earth supply and port fees depend on. What to watch: USTR notices putting the MFN cuts into effect (none were in the Federal Register as of Sept. 28); Chinese farm-purchase data; the flow of rare-earth export licences; and the first talks on AI incidents, due by the end of November. Our view: the exclusion of strategic sectors means tech decoupling continues regardless.

DOT finalized a reset of fuel-economy (CAFE) rules to 34.9 mpg by 2031 instead of 50.4: truck-heavy Detroit gains, while EV makers' credit income and drivers' fuel bills loseAutos / Regulation

On Monday the Transportation Department released its 892-page final rule, named 'Freedom Means Affordable Cars'. It rewrites Corporate Average Fuel Economy standards for model years 2022–2031 (Roll Call, HNGN). The fleet target for 2031 drops to 34.9 mpg from the Biden-era 50.4 mpg, about 31% lower (our arithmetic). Required gains shrink to up to 1% a year from 2%, and the 2024 fleet averaged 30.1 mpg (NPR, Roll Call).

DOT claims the rule cuts the average new-vehicle price by $1,300 and saves consumers $138bn over five years. The Environmental Defense Fund counters that drivers will spend about $1,600 more on gasoline, a figure it says appears in NHTSA's own impact analysis (Roll Call). Enforcement was already weak: the July 2025 budget law set CAFE civil penalties to zero. HNGN reports the rule also scraps trading of compliance credits between automakers.

Roll Call says the practical effect starts with model year 2028. The timing is awkward: AAA puts regular gasoline near $4.50 a gallon and diesel near $6.50 amid the Iran war (NPR). Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy called the rule relief for families and a boost to manufacturing (NPR). The Alliance for Automotive Innovation's John Bozzella called it a course correction from rules that effectively forced a switch to EVs (NPR, Roll Call).

Critics at the Center for Biological Diversity, EDF and the Zero Emission Transportation Association warned of more fuel use, more pollution and weaker competitiveness (NPR). Tesla shares fell after the announcement (Roll Call). Winners: GM, Ford and Stellantis, whose fleets lean on trucks and SUVs (NPR notes Stellantis's pledge of $13bn in US investment over four years). Losers: EV makers that sold compliance credits, and households' fuel costs over the life of a vehicle.

Why it matters: buyers get the lower sticker price up front, but the higher fuel costs build up over years of ownership, at pump prices already inflated by the war. Losing credit income also tilts the economics against EV makers. Our view: slightly higher US gasoline demand is a small plus for refiners. What to watch: publication in the Federal Register (not listed as of Sept. 28); lawsuits from states and environmental groups; matching EPA tailpipe-rule changes; automakers' model-year 2028 product plans; and the regulatory-credit revenue line in Tesla's Q3 results (forecast).

On the same day, Putin signed a decree adding 15,500 personnel, which lifts the armed forces' authorized strength to 2,441,630 (Kyiv Independent). Winners: defence-industrial plants and their workers. Why it matters: Moscow is budgeting for high-intensity war through 2027 despite ceasefire diplomacy. With oil-and-gas income revised down, the burden shifts to borrowing, reserves and one-off taxes, a model that only works while domestic banks keep buying government bonds (our view). What to watch: the Oct. 1 draft and how much of it is classified; demand at Russian government bond (OFZ) auctions and the Bank of Russia's rate path; and whether windfall levies spread to other sectors (forecast).
