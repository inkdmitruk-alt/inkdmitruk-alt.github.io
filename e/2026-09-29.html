🧭 The throughline

The bond market is setting the pace. Long yields at 2002–2007 highs are now pulling on everything else: consumer confidence, IPO windows, gold, even the Fed's own messaging. The inflation impulse comes mainly from energy and the Iran war, but the fiscal side is making it worse, with deficits near 6% of GDP and AI labs signing up for hundreds of billions in compute, all competing with Treasury for the same capital. Equities are holding flat only because a narrow AI complex keeps producing mega-deals, and that leaves the index exposed to any disappointment in this week's PCE and payrolls data. Williams pointed markets toward a late-year hike rather than October. If they take that cue, the short end may ease while the long end stays heavy, a bear-steepening pattern that hurts housing, small caps and long-duration growth stocks.

Consumer confidence collapses to 81.9, the lowest since 2014, as inflation expectations climb to 6.1%Conference Board / Reuters / Quartz

The Conference Board index fell 6.7 points to 81.9 in September, well below the 89.2 Reuters consensus. The Present Situation index dropped 7.9 to 109.3. The Expectations index fell 5.9 to 63.6, its third straight decline. Average 12-month inflation expectations rose 0.3 pp to 6.1%, with the median at 5.1%. For the first time since the question was introduced four years ago, more respondents called their family finances 'bad' than 'good'.

The survey ran Sept. 1–23, spanning the Fed hike. Separately, August JOLTS showed 7.097M openings against 7.228M expected, and 30-year mortgage rates reached 7.58%, the highest since November 2023 (Yahoo Finance).

Why it matters: rising inflation expectations alongside weakening sentiment is the stagflation mix the Fed dreads. It argues for both caution and vigilance, which is exactly the balance Williams struck.

🚢 Trade, Tariffs & Policy 3 items

US ban on Canadian alcohol, dairy, molasses and big motorcycles takes effectCBC / CNBC

Just after midnight on Sept. 29, the US began blocking imports of specified Canadian alcoholic beverages, dairy products including whey, molasses and large-engine motorcycles, about $1B in trade. It follows 50% US tariffs imposed this summer on roughly $20B of Canadian goods, which Washington justified by pointing to Canadian treatment of US dairy, autos and alcohol. The US targeted Canadian alcohol after provinces pulled American liquor from store shelves.

Canada has retaliated dollar-for-dollar with 15–50% tariffs on C$27.6B ($19.45B) of US goods, including steel, dairy, farm equipment and appliances.

Why it matters: the dollar amounts are modest, but outright bans are a qualitative escalation beyond tariffs, adding uncertainty to North American supply chains and the USMCA framework.

Sources: CBC: U.S. bans on Canadian products take effect · CNBC: Canadian import ban comes into force

Jamie Dimon pitches a 'big, beautiful' US-Europe free trade deal, conditional on EU reformsWSJ op-ed (via CNBC, Quartz, Benzinga)

In a WSJ op-ed published Monday, the JPMorgan CEO wrote that if Europe executes meaningful economic and military reforms, 'the U.S. will negotiate one big, beautiful economic and free-trade agreement with Europe.' He said the pact could extend to Canada, Mexico, Japan, South Korea, Australia and the Philippines. He urged the EU to complete its Capital Markets Union and Banking Union and to implement the Draghi and Letta reports, noting under 16% of their measures have been adopted.

Dimon called it a 'game changer' that would let allies 'set the global rules on trade' in the face of autocratic pressure.

Why it matters: this is a prominent Wall Street voice offering the White House an alternative to tariff-by-tariff bilateralism. Watch whether administration officials pick it up.

Sources: CNBC: Jamie Dimon's plan for a big deal with Europe · Quartz: Dimon calls for US-Europe free trade deal

Treasury will auto-enroll 60M+ children in Trump Accounts starting as early as Oct. 1CNBC / Bloomberg Law

Temporary regulations published Tuesday replace the opt-in process (IRS Form 4547 or TrumpAccounts.gov) with automatic enrollment. Treasury says this will create accounts for more than 60 million additional children under 18. Families can contribute up to $5,000 a year, of which up to $2,500 can come from employers. Children born in 2025–2028 are eligible for a one-time $1,000 government contribution. Auto-enrollment does not trigger that $1,000 on its own, because taxpayers must elect it under last year's tax law.

Why it matters: tens of millions of new investment accounts could become a steady, price-insensitive source of equity inflows over time, and a political talking point before the midterms.
