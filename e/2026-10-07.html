Fed minutes: 'most participants' expect another hike by year-end after September's unanimous 25bp move to 3.75-4.00%Reuters / CNBC

Minutes of the September 15-16 FOMC meeting, released at 2pm ET, show the committee voted unanimously for a quarter-point hike to 3.75-4.00%, the first increase in more than three years, and that most participants judged 'another increase in the target range ... would likely be appropriate by year end.' There was no signal on timing: participants said they 'approached each meeting with an open mind.' The hawkish logic was risk management: 'many participants' argued a higher rate path is prudent insurance against persistent inflation from strong demand or supply shocks (energy, tariffs), with underlying inflation still 'more than a percentage point' above 2%.

But the committee is split on what the hike means. Some saw it as precautionary, to stop energy price shocks from spreading; a more hawkish group saw emerging demand-driven inflation; 'a couple' tied it to a higher neutral rate; and 'several participants' said current policy is 'not restrictive or only mildly restrictive,' which cuts both ways. Data cited: August PCE 3.4% y/y, core 3.0% (both matched July), September payrolls +29,000, the slowest of the year, unemployment 4.2%, Q2 GDP revised up to 2.2%.

Reuters notes dissents are possible at the October 27-28 meeting, which falls days before the midterms. CME FedWatch puts an October hold at ~81% (up from ~54% a month ago) and a December 8-9 hike largely priced. MarketWatch's read: 'no appetite for a series of interest-rate hikes.'

Why it matters: the Fed has shifted from pausing to a one-more-hike bias while the labor market stalls at +29K payrolls, a stagflation-flavored setup. If December is the next move, the front end is anchored, but the long end (30-year 5.70%) is being driven by oil and supply, not the Fed. Watch the October 14 CPI and whether October produces a split vote.

NY Fed survey: one-year inflation expectations jump to 3.9%, highest since May 2023; households more pessimistic on finances, credit harderNY Fed / Reuters

The New York Fed's September Survey of Consumer Expectations, released Wednesday, shows median one-year inflation expectations up 0.3pp to 3.9%, the highest since May 2023, three-year expectations up 0.1pp to 3.3% and five-year unchanged at 3.0%. Expected price increases rose in every category tracked: gasoline 4.8%, food 5.5%, rent 6.8%, medical care 9.2% and college 7.5% (+1.4pp).

Households expect spending to grow 5.5% (+0.3pp, also a high since May 2023) while expected earnings growth fell 0.3pp to 2.6%, a real-income squeeze that explains why the share reporting or expecting a worse financial situation rose and the net share finding credit harder to get increased. The labor-market readings actually improved: the perceived probability of higher unemployment in a year fell to 43.9%, job-loss probability to 13.5%, and the chance of finding a job if laid off rose to 46.1%.

The mean probability that US stocks are higher in 12 months fell to 40.2%. Separately, CPI data cited by CNBC show airfares up 23.4% year over year on fuel costs, one of the most visible pass-throughs of the diesel/jet-fuel spike.

Why it matters: expectations at 3.9% with earnings expectations at 2.6% is the household version of stagflation, and it is exactly the de-anchoring the hawkish FOMC camp warned about in the minutes. It raises the bar for the Fed to stay on hold in December even if payrolls keep weakening.

Reuters, citing Sandeep Bajoria of the International Sunflower Oil Association (and CEO of Mumbai broker Sunvin), reports that a 20,000-tonne Russian sunflower-oil shipment to India was cancelled because the supplier could not deliver after damage to Black Sea port infrastructure, a cancellation Bajoria said has not been seen in many years, and that roughly 60,000 tonnes more are delayed.

Why it matters: this is the first hard evidence that the port war is breaking vegetable-oil supply chains, not just grain. Ukraine allows partial gas exports to the EU for the first time since 2022: up to 15% of a producer's prior-month output, via auctions, with a crisis-committee kill switchEkonomichna Pravda

The Cabinet of Ministers on October 7 approved the first gas exports since the March 4, 2022 ban: each private producer may export up to 15% of the volume it delivered to the transmission system the previous month (e.g., 100 mcm delivered in October allows 15 mcm exported in November, no carry-over), sold through auctions on the Ukrainian Energy Exchange open to non-residents. Production-sharing gas and PSO suppliers (Naftogaz, Ukrgasvydobuvannya, Ukrnafta, Ukrnaftoburinnya) are excluded, so the beneficiaries are private producers such as DTEK Naftogaz, Burisma, Geo-Alliance, Zakhidnadraservis, Poltava Oil and Gas (JKX) and Nadra-Geoinvest.

ExPro estimates eligible volumes at roughly 250-260 mcm a month. The economics: the EU price on October 7 was $888 per 1,000 m3 versus UAH16,037 (~$356) for Ukrainian industrial buyers, a 2.5x gap, and private output has fallen from ~5 bcm in 2021 to 3.7 bcm in 2025. Storage stands at 15.6 bcm, 1 bcm above the 14.6 bcm government target. The Energy Ministry's crisis committee can halt new auctions on request from the ministry, Naftogaz or the TSO if energy security is threatened; gas already sold can still be delivered. Market participants had expected this to wait until spring 2027.

Why it matters: it is an FX-earning, production-incentive move timed to high European prices and low EU storage, but the author's warning stands: selling at $888 in autumn and buying back at a winter peak would be a policy loss. The size (~250 mcm/month) is small for Europe but a meaningful signal that Ukraine is positioning as a gas exporter despite the strikes.

🎙 Trump & Policy Signals 3 items

Trump's dyed-diesel order: 24.4c/gal federal excise tax deferred (not forgiven) for on-road use through Dec 31; White House claims 'up to $100 per fill' as diesel sits at $6.32Time / CNBC / NPR

The executive order signed October 5 temporarily waives the 'off-road only' rule for tax-exempt red-dyed diesel, allowing truckers and farmers to burn it on public highways with the 24.4 cents-per-gallon federal excise tax deferred from October 5 through December 31; the tax obligation is pushed into next year, and the order directs officials to explore forgiving it, potentially via legislation.

The White House's 'What They Are Saying' release says the move delivers 'immediate diesel relief, saving Americans up to $100 per fill.' The price backdrop, per AAA: diesel rose from $3.76 a gallon on February 27, before the Iran war, to a record $6.53 on September 22 and was $6.32 on Tuesday; regular gasoline is about $4.36. Ten states (including Texas, Oklahoma, Louisiana, Indiana) had already allowed some on-road dyed-diesel use and Iowa suspended penalties for ag hauls.

Skepticism is broad: GasBuddy's Patrick De Haan said 'It might lead to savings for some, but there are a lot of details that will prevent this from having widespread relief'; industry groups note it does not remove state taxes or environmental restrictions, and CNBC's headline reads that the order 'exposes White House limits to counter surging fuel prices' because nothing in it adds supply.

NPR explains the fuel is normally restricted to farm and construction equipment. The IEA's September report put US diesel above $200/bbl in early September, 94% over pre-war.

Why it matters: a 24-cent deferral against a $2.56-per-gallon war premium is a signaling device for the November 3 midterms, not a price fix, and it costs the Highway Trust Fund revenue. The real levers are a diesel export ban (Trump has said it could raise gasoline prices) and the IEA diesel-stock release now being accelerated.
