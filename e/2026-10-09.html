📊 Macro & Rates 4 items

10-year yield retreats from a 24-year high of 5.364% to ~5.22–5.25% after Trump's Iran pledgeFXStreet / Reuters via Investing.com / NBC News

The 10-year Treasury yield touched 5.364% on Wednesday, its highest since 2002, as the bond selloff that has run for weeks extended. On Thursday it fell to its lowest level since the prior Friday — roughly 5.22% — after Trump posted that the US would not attack Iran before the midterms. On Friday morning it was steady near 5.25%. The transmission channel is oil: a lower geopolitical premium in crude means lower expected headline inflation, which in turn trims the odds of further Fed tightening.

But the move is modest relative to the run-up, and the curve is still pricing a world of persistently expensive money. With the fed funds range at 3.75–4.00% after September's hike, the long end is effectively doing additional tightening on the Fed's behalf.

Why it matters: every 10 bp at the long end flows straight into mortgages, corporate refinancing and equity valuations; a decisive break back above 5.3% would revive pressure on growth stocks and housing.

Sources: FXStreet · Reuters via Investing.com · NBC News

30-year mortgage rate jumps 12 bp to 7.4%, first time above that level since November 2023The Hill / Freddie Mac

Freddie Mac's weekly survey showed the average 30-year fixed rate at 7.4%, up 12 basis points in a week and the highest since November 2023. Mortgage rates track the 10-year Treasury plus a spread, so the bond selloff is now visibly hitting household finances. At 7.4%, monthly payments on a median-priced home are materially higher than a year ago, which freezes existing owners with low-rate loans in place and suppresses inventory.

Homebuilders and mortgage-sensitive lenders are the most exposed equities. The timing is politically awkward: housing affordability is a top voter issue three and a half weeks before the midterms.

Why it matters: housing is the most rate-sensitive part of the economy; a sustained 7%+ mortgage environment drags on construction, furniture, appliances and local tax revenues into 2027.

Sources: The Hill

Bessent's new adviser Zervos: yields are 'really, really high' but can fall soon; Treasury also adds Fed critic Judy SheltonCNBC

David Zervos, the veteran Jefferies strategist who recently joined Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent as an adviser, said Treasury yields are 'really, really high' but could come down soon, comments made as the 10- and 30-year yields sat at 24-year highs. Separately, CNBC reports Bessent has hired Judy Shelton — the gold-standard advocate whose Fed board nomination the Senate blocked in 2020 — as a counselor.

Together the hires signal a Treasury increasingly vocal about rates and openly sceptical of Fed orthodoxy. Markets will watch for any shift in debt-issuance strategy, such as leaning more on bills than coupons, which would be the most direct lever Treasury has over long yields.

Why it matters: if Treasury tilts issuance toward short-dated bills to relieve the long end, it could cap 10-year yields — at the cost of higher rollover risk and potential friction with the Fed.

Sources: CNBC — Zervos · CNBC — Shelton

Labor market cooling into the CPI test: September payrolls +29K, unemployment 4.2%, record margin debtTrading Economics / CNBC

The backdrop for rates is a labor market that has nearly stalled: September payrolls rose just 29,000 against forecasts of roughly 84,000–95,000, unemployment ticked up to 4.2%, July–August were revised down by a combined 60,000 and wage growth slowed to 3.0% y/y, the weakest since May 2021. Leverage of that kind amplifies drawdowns: Thursday's AI selloff was the type of shock that can trigger margin calls. The September CPI release on October 14 is the next major macro catalyst.

Why it matters: if CPI shows energy feeding into core prices, the Fed's 'one more hike' bias hardens even as jobs weaken — the worst mix for both bonds and equities.

Diesel tax order published: 24.4¢/gal federal excise deferred Oct 5–Dec 31, red diesel allowed on highways pending IRS guidanceFederal Register / Akin Gump / Energy Marketers of America

The Federal Register on October 9 published Trump's 'Emergency Tax Relief on Diesel Fuel' order, signed October 5. It directs Treasury to determine whether it can legally defer the 24.4-cent-per-gallon federal diesel excise and waive penalties for on-highway use of untaxed dyed ('red') diesel from October 5 through December 31, 2026. Crucially, it is a deferral, not forgiveness: Section 4 only asks Treasury to explore ways, including legislation, to eliminate the deferred liability.

The White House estimates savings of about $60 per 250-gallon fill-up, or $100+ where states also suspend taxes; state excises are unaffected. GasBuddy's Patrick De Haan expects limited broad relief. The order is a pre-midterm response to $100+ crude and elevated diesel prices.

Why it matters: trucking and farm costs could ease at the margin once IRS guidance lands, but consumer gasoline prices are untouched — and a deferred tax that is never forgiven becomes a 2027 liability for carriers.

Sources: Federal Register · Akin Gump · EMA Regulatory Report
