📊 Macro & Rates 4 items

September PPI +1.3% m/m and +6.3% y/y, the largest 12-month rise since December 2022BLS / Globe and Mail

The BLS producer price index for final demand rose 1.3% in September against a 0.5% consensus, and 6.3% over 12 months, the biggest annual gain since 6.4% in December 2022. More than 80% of the monthly increase came from final-demand energy, up 10.8%, a direct pass-through of the Hormuz crisis and $100+ crude. Core PPI ex food, energy and trade services rose 0.5% m/m and 5.0% y/y; the narrower ex-food-and-energy measure rose 0.7% versus 0.3% expected.

The print landed one day after September CPI showed +0.6% m/m and 3.7% y/y (3.6% expected) with core at 2.6%. Retail sales rose 0.8% (0.3% expected) and 0.9% ex-autos, so the consumer is absorbing the price shock for now; initial jobless claims were 199,000. The Cleveland Fed nowcast already has October CPI near 3.8%.

Why it matters: producer prices lead consumer goods prices by one to three months, and energy-driven PPI feeds transport and food. Unless crude breaks lower, the Fed's 'wait and see' stance gets harder to defend into the Oct. 27–28 meeting.

Sources: BLS · The Globe and Mail

Why it matters: the Fed is being asked to hike into an equity drawdown and a credit scare. A hike would validate the bond market's inflation fear; a hold would be read as political capitulation. Either path raises volatility into year-end.

⚡ Energy & Commodities 3 items

Brent $104.02 (−1.9%), WTI $94.04 (−1.1%) as Washington weighs easing shadow-fleet sanctionsReuters / Yahoo Finance / EnergyNow

Crude slipped from Wednesday's ~$106/$95 after reports that the Trump administration is considering easing restrictions on 'shadow fleet' tankers that have carried Russian, Iranian and Venezuelan oil, in order to bring down record freight rates; no decision has been made. The downside was capped by the Strait of Hormuz, where a container ship and a dry-bulk carrier sank within 24 hours after being hit by projectiles, by Saudi-Houthi fighting that threatens Bab el-Mandeb, and by Ukraine resuming strikes on Russian refineries.

The IEA puts global observed inventories 509 million barrels below pre-conflict levels, sees diesel markets constrained into 2027, and forecasts 2026 global demand down 2.6 mb/d; Russian output rose 65 kb/d to 8.43 mb/d in September but remains 870 kb/d below a year ago, while Middle East exports recovered to ~75% of late-February levels. Draft EU summit conclusions add that the European Council 'regrets the United States' decision to temporarily lift sanctions on Russian diesel.'

Why it matters: sanctions relief on tankers would cut delivered-crude costs and diesel cracks within weeks and could knock $5–8 off Brent — while handing the Russian budget a windfall and widening the U.S.–EU rift on Ukraine. The opposite risk is refinery strikes tightening an already short diesel market.

Sources: Yahoo Finance · EnergyNow · FXEmpire

U.S. crude stocks −11.1M barrels (API) after Hurricane Isaias shut Gulf output; EIA due todayAPI / Yahoo Finance

The American Petroleum Institute reported U.S. crude inventories fell 11.12 million barrels in the week to Oct. 9, against expectations for a ~1.9 million-barrel build — one of the largest weekly draws on record, attributed to Hurricane Isaias temporarily shutting much of Gulf Coast production. The official EIA report follows later Thursday. A draw of this size, if confirmed, tightens the Cushing balance just as refiners head into turnaround season and diesel is already short globally. It also helps explain why WTI (−1.1%) fell less than Brent (−1.9%) on the sanctions-relief headline.

Why it matters: the Brent-WTI spread is narrowing on a U.S.-specific supply shock. If EIA confirms the draw, U.S. gasoline and diesel retail prices — already a midterm issue — get another leg up into November.

Sources: Yahoo Finance

Russia's oil-and-gas revenue −17.2% y/y over Jan–Sep despite Urals above $92; deficit heading to ~3% of GDPReuters via Ekonomichna Pravda / Xinhua

Federal oil-and-gas receipts fell 17.2% to 5.471 trillion rubles in the first nine months of 2026 even though Urals traded above $92 at end-September, roughly double pre-war levels. The Finance Ministry blames a stronger ruble and higher compensation payments to domestic refineries as global product prices rose — i.e. the refinery-strike campaign is costing Moscow twice, in lost throughput and in subsidies.

Reuters calculations cited by Ekonomichna Pravda point to a higher October take on one-off tax timing, but the Oct. 4 budget guidelines project oil-and-gas revenue sliding from 3.3% of GDP in 2026 to 2.7% by 2029 and the 2026 deficit near 3% of GDP, almost twice the plan. The Bank of Russia will also ban direct peer-to-peer crypto trades from July 2027, closing one capital-flight channel.

Why it matters: this is the economic channel of the war that markets price — Russia is funding itself through domestic taxes and borrowing while hydrocarbon rents shrink. Any U.S. shadow-fleet relief directly reverses that trend.
