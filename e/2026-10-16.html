Warsh withholds a rate signal before Oct 27-28 FOMC, praises global tighteningCNBC / Yahoo Finance / Benzinga

Speaking with IMF chief Kristalina Georgieva at the IMF-World Bank Annual Meetings in Bangkok, Fed Chair Kevin Warsh declined to preview the Oct 27-28 decision. He praised the recent wave of global monetary tightening, framing it as 'no free riders' among central banks. On AI, he said the path will be 'very bumpy' but ultimately good for wealth and productivity, and argued AI investment is currently a demand shock that adds near-term inflation pressure before supply benefits arrive.

He admitted uncertainty over how deeply AI will affect employment. Market pricing cited by outlets puts October hold odds between roughly 72% and 87%, with CME implying about 15% odds of another hike; traders lean toward a 25bp hike in December. The Fed will hold the first field hearing of its productivity and jobs task force on Nov. 16.

Why it matters: a Fed that sees AI as inflationary demand rather than disinflationary supply is less likely to rescue richly valued tech, and keeps December hike risk alive.

September CPI: headline 3.7% y/y, core 2.6%, both above forecastsBLS via Trading Economics / FT Portfolios / XTB

The CPI released Oct 14 showed headline prices up 0.6% m/m and 3.7% y/y versus 3.6% expected. Core CPI rose 0.3% m/m against a 0.2% consensus, lifting the annual core rate to 2.6% from a five-year low of 2.4% in August. Gasoline jumped 9.1% in the month and accounted for more than half of the headline gain, a direct pass-through from the Gulf supply shock. Rents rose 0.3%, continuing a cooling trend, but airfares climbed 1.2% m/m and 22.4% y/y as jet fuel costs feed through. Analysts described it as a modest but meaningful hawkish surprise that keeps inflation above the 2% target for longer.

Why it matters: energy-driven headline inflation is leaking into transport services, which is exactly the second-round effect that keeps the Fed in hiking mode.

Sources: Trading Economics: US core inflation above forecasts · First Trust: CPI rose 0.6% in September · InvestingLive: US September CPI 3.7% vs 3.6% expected

Labour market stalls: 29k September payrolls, unemployment 4.2%BabyPips / BLS

Nonfarm payrolls rose only about 29,000 in September versus forecasts of roughly 84,000-95,000, and July and August were revised down by a combined 60,000. The unemployment rate ticked up to 4.2% from 4.1%, partly because more people entered the labour force. Average hourly earnings rose just 0.1% m/m to $37.81, a 3.0% annual pace, now below headline inflation of 3.7% — meaning real wages are falling.

Jobless claims remain low, so the data point to slower hiring rather than mass layoffs. This combination of weak jobs and hot prices is the textbook stagflation dilemma for the Fed.

Why it matters: negative real wage growth squeezes consumer spending, while the Fed cannot ease because inflation is rising — a headwind for retailers and consumer credit.

Hormuz: at least one ship attack a day since Oct 2, insurance and freight under pressureBaird Maritime / Reuters-syndicated / BBC

The UK Maritime Trade Operations agency has reported at least one attack a day in the Strait of Hormuz or the Gulf of Aden since Oct 2, and the BBC reports attack reports have hit a record high. Shipping intelligence firm Marisks counted at least seven incidents in one week, including the VLCC Kazimah III hit on Oct 1 and the Aframax Lipsi struck on Oct 4. As of Oct 14, a container ship and a tanker had been struck within the strait, while US Central Command said it helped move more than 19 million barrels of crude through the strait in a single day.

Marisks describes a 'heightened and increasingly unpredictable kinetic threat' as traffic rises. Each incident raises war-risk insurance premiums and tanker freight rates, which are added to delivered crude costs.

Why it matters: even when barrels keep flowing, attack frequency sets the risk premium in Brent and in shipping stocks.

Vortexa's 14-day average for Middle East crude and condensate hit 18.6 million bpd, above the 10-year seasonal average. Pre-war exports averaged close to 18 million bpd between March 2025 and February 2026. Most of the increase came from Saudi Arabia, which is pushing volume to regain market share.

The fact that Brent still trades above $100 despite recovered volumes shows the price is driven by risk premium and inventory rebuilding rather than a pure barrel shortage. Separately, a report noted the BBC framing that exports are slowing as attacks rise, so the October trend should be watched.

Why it matters: if volumes hold while attacks fade, the war premium in oil could unwind quickly; if attacks cut flows, the downside for inflation is limited.

US Treasury waiver permits Russian diesel transactions until April 7, 2027Deccan Herald / News9

The US Treasury issued a temporary sanctions waiver authorising transactions in Russian diesel through April 7, 2027 — a product-specific easing, not a removal of broader Russia sanctions. President Trump presented it as a way to bring down soaring fuel costs, announcing that Russia would release millions of tonnes of diesel. The waiver redirects Russian product toward buyers previously deterred by US secondary-sanction risk, which could narrow global diesel cracks.

Lawmakers from both parties have criticised a deal that could channel about $4 billion to Moscow. Trump has also criticised Ukraine's strikes on Russian refineries, which are a key reason Russian output is constrained.

Why it matters: this is a rare sanctions loosening during the war, and it signals the White House now prioritises pump prices ahead of the midterms over maximum pressure on Moscow.

The White House released two product lists for potential tariff reductions under the new US-China Board of Trade mechanism: one covering Chinese goods imported into the US and one covering US goods imported into China. Covington expects the Board will not offer relief from Section 232 national-security tariffs or from antidumping and countervailing duties. 15% Section 232 tariff on polysilicon from Dec 4, plus minimum import pricesMorgan Lewis

President Trump announced that effective Dec 4 the US will impose a 15% tariff on polysilicon and derivative products, combined with minimum import prices. Commerce has also imposed temporary anti-stockpiling restrictions, and CBP may bar affected importers from further entries before Dec 4 to prevent front-loading. Polysilicon is the base material for solar cells and semiconductors, so the duty raises input costs for US solar installers and module assemblers.

Domestic polysilicon producers stand to benefit from both the tariff and the price floor. The action fits the administration's pattern of using Section 232 after the IEEPA tariffs were struck down.

Why it matters: higher solar input costs at a time of high power demand from AI data centres could slow cheap capacity additions.

Legal backdrop: post-IEEPA tariff wall rests on Section 301 (10-12.5% on 60 economies) and 232Tax Policy Center / Baker McKenzie

The Supreme Court struck down the IEEPA-based tariffs in February 2026, so the administration has rebuilt its tariff wall on other statutes. On July 23, 2026, USTR finalised Section 301 tariffs of 10-12.5% on imports from 60 economies (59 countries plus the EU), with lower rates for partners that signed trade agreements. Section 232 sector tariffs on metals, and now polysilicon, sit on top.

In Europe, the Parliament has approved legislation implementing EU tariff commitments under the EU-US deal, with Council approval pending. The diesel dispute is a risk to that final EU step.

Why it matters: tariffs are now more legally durable than in 2025, so companies should treat them as a structural cost rather than a negotiating bluff.
