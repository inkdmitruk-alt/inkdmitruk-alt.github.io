📊 Macro & Rates 5 items

September CPI 3.7% y/y, hotter than expected; PPI +1.3% m/m, the biggest since 2022BLS / CNBC / CBS

Headline CPI rose 0.6% m/m and 3.7% y/y against a 3.6% forecast (August: 3.4%); core was 0.3% m/m and 2.6% y/y, a tick above consensus. The driver is energy: gasoline +34.2% y/y, fuel oil +74.2%, airfares +22.4%. Producer prices jumped 1.3% m/m and 6.3% y/y, the largest annual gain since December 2022, with final-demand goods +2.6% m/m — a series record — and energy +10.8% contributing over 80% of the move.

Average hourly earnings rose only 0.1% m/m and 3% y/y, so real wages are falling. Import prices are up 8.1% y/y and export prices 11.6%, mostly fuel. The mechanism is a classic supply shock: energy costs flow from producer to consumer prices with a lag of one to three months.

Why it matters: core inflation is still contained, which is the Fed's argument for patience, but pipeline pressure in PPI points to higher Q4 CPI prints (forecast). Watch September PCE and the Michigan inflation-expectations survey.

Sources: CNBC — CPI · BLS — PPI · CBS News

Why it matters: the gap between ~15% market pricing and a credible hike risk is an asymmetric setup — a surprise would hit long bonds, tech and crypto simultaneously (scenario, not a forecast).

🚢 Trade, Tariffs & Policy 4 items

Graham Act waiver: 500% duties on Russian goods and up to 100% on top buyers of Russian energy shelvedReuters / Moscow Times / NBC / Kyiv Independent

On the night of Oct 16 Trump signed an order under the Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act of 2026, two days before its measures were due to take effect on Oct 18. The order formally sets 500% duties on Russian goods and up to 100% on goods from the five largest importers of Russian oil and gas — including China and India — and then waives them in the national interest, with no expiry.

Existing sanctions under earlier orders remain, and a ban on buying Russian sovereign debt has applied since Sept 18. Russia's Kirill Dmitriev called it a historic moment. Republicans including Thom Tillis (beyond irresponsible) and John Cornyn criticized it; the Senate had passed the law 86–11.

Why it matters: for markets this removes a tariff shock on China and India and a further oil spike weeks before the midterms — disinflationary near term, but it raises the odds of congressional pushback and policy volatility.

Sources: Reuters via US News · Moscow Times · The Hill

Russian diesel deal and OFAC licence through April 2027; Meeks bill seeks to revoke itCNBC / NBC / Kyiv Independent

On Oct 9 Trump said Russia would supply more than 4 million tonnes of diesel to world markets: 300,000 tonnes immediately, 500,000 in November, then 1 million and up to 3 million more depending on refinery conditions; CNBC later sized it at about 4.8 million tonnes, or ~36 million barrels. Treasury issued a general licence permitting purchases and imports of Russian diesel, including into the US, until April 7, 2027.

Goldman expects diesel to stay expensive into 2027, and there is no confirmation that cargoes are already moving. Rep. Gregory Meeks introduced a bipartisan bill to block the deal, and a bipartisan group of senators urged Trump to rescind it.

Why it matters: the licence is the policy foundation for the Graham Act waiver; if cargoes materialize they would ease distillate cracks, but the political cost is rising in Congress.

Sources: CNBC · CNBC — by the numbers · Kyiv Independent — Meeks bill

IEEPA tariff refunds: $130–145 billion returned to importers; customs revenue −11%CNN / CBO

Following the Supreme Court ruling that struck down most IEEPA-based tariffs, the government refunded $130–145 billion in FY2026, by CBO and Treasury estimates respectively. In June, refunds ($50 billion) exceeded gross customs collections ($24 billion). Net customs receipts fell 11% to about $173 billion. For importers the refunds were a cash windfall; for the Treasury they widened the deficit. The administration's tariff leverage now rests on Sections 232 and 301, which require slower, product-specific processes.

Why it matters: tariff policy has become slower and narrower, so trade threats now carry less immediate market impact than in 2025 — but refunds should fade in 2027, helping the deficit at the margin.

Sources: CNN · CBO Monthly Budget Review

Both moves point to energy and industrial policy increasingly being run through trade channels.

Why it matters: faster LNG approvals support US gas exporters' long-term contracts, while a UK–EU alignment on Chinese EVs would broaden Western trade friction with Beijing.
