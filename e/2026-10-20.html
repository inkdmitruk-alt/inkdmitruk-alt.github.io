🧭 The throughline

The US economy is caught between an energy shock and a cooling labor market. Brent touched $111 on unverified Houthi strike claims before reversing, diesel is above $6 a gallon, and 10-year yields sit near 5.1% while September payrolls added only 29,000 jobs. Markets have mostly priced out an October Fed hike but still see December as live, so every oil spike now passes straight into rate expectations, mortgage costs and bank stocks. Washington is trying to cap fuel prices before the November 3 midterms: an $8 billion diesel relief program for farmers and a waiver of the Graham Act's Russia sanctions both work through supply and subsidies rather than demand. Meanwhile, the financing behind the AI buildout is showing strain in credit markets (CoreWeave's bonds trade like distressed debt, and a Korean data-center IPO fell 32%), even as a handful of megacaps keep the indexes up.

📊 Macro & Rates 4 items

October Fed hike mostly priced out; December stays liveFed

The Fed raised rates by 25bp to 3.75–4.00% on September 16. The next decision is October 27–28. Bank of America notes that about 85% of market participants expect no change next week, though BofA economists still see an October hike as possible. The September minutes showed most officials expect another increase by year-end. Fed staff estimated headline PCE at 3.8% and core at 3.4% in the year to August, and do not see inflation reaching 2% until 2029.

Last week Williams and Collins both called one more 2026 hike appropriate. Williams added that energy and tech investment are behind the near-term price pressure and that he sees no need for hikes in 2027. CPI ran at 3.7% year on year in the latest print.

Why it matters: The reaction function now runs through oil. Williams's benign 2027 view assumes energy stops adding to inflation, so another sustained move toward $110 Brent would make a December hike close to certain.

Bank stocks on their longest losing streak since 2008Financials

The US bank index is on its longest losing streak since 2008 and is down about 11% since early September, according to Yahoo Finance's market blog. Why it matters: Lenders are the transmission belt between rates and the real economy. A sustained bank drawdown usually comes before tighter lending standards, which would add to the Fed's tightening without another hike.

Diesel at about $6.32 a gallon is the real squeeze pointRefined products

AAA's diesel average is about $6.32 a gallon, compared with $3.63 a year ago, according to reporting on the new farm relief program. Diesel hit an all-time high earlier in the Iran war and kept climbing. Jet fuel costs have pushed airlines to cut flights and raise fares. The US Strategic Petroleum Reserve fell below 300 million barrels in early August, down more than 100 million barrels since the start of 2026, which limits Washington's ability to cushion further shocks. Refined-product shipments from the Gulf are recovering more slowly than crude.

Why it matters: Diesel moves freight, harvest and construction costs, so it passes into core goods prices with a lag. This is the channel through which energy becomes stubborn inflation rather than a one-off headline spike.

Russia sanctions waiver adds expected supply; analysts call it a midterm price playSanctions

On Friday, October 16, Trump signed a national-interest waiver of the Graham Act's Russia provisions two days before they took effect. The law would have imposed tariffs of up to 500% on Russian goods and up to 100% on the top buyers of Russian crude and gas. The waiver followed a deal allowing Russia to sell diesel to the US and world markets amid shortages. Brent fell about 2% on Monday as traders priced in more Russian fuel exports.

Rubio said the administration will take up to 60 days to assess the sanctions' impact on the global economy. Phillip Nova's Priyanka Sachdeva called the waiver an attempt to keep oil prices in check before the midterms that does little for physical Hormuz disruptions.

Why it matters: The waiver removes a secondary-tariff threat that hung over buyers of Russian oil such as India, China and Turkey. That lowers tail risk for global refined-product supply but weakens Western leverage over Moscow's oil revenue.

Sources: CNBC · Business Recorder

$8 billion Harvest Fuel Relief program for farmersFiscal

Trump announced the program at a Kansas rally on October 19 and on Truth Social, framing it as $8 billion to offset diesel costs during harvest. USDA describes one-time payments covering part of modeled 2026 crop-year losses. The formula uses planted acres reported to the Farm Service Agency, Economic Research Service cost-of-production estimates, and WASDE yields and prices. Funding comes through the Commodity Credit Corporation.

Payment rates for Title I commodities come in November, with other crops later. It follows the earlier $12 billion Farmer Bridge Assistance program paid to more than 560,000 farmers.

Why it matters: Farm support since the trade war is now over $20 billion, paid through a fund the Agriculture Secretary controls without new appropriations. That is quasi-permanent fiscal support at a time when the Fed is still fighting inflation.

Sources: USDA · The Hill

Russia sanctions waiver draws bipartisan backlashSanctions politics

The waiver covers every operative Russia section of the Graham Act. The White House certified it as in the national interest but gave no further explanation. Republican Sen. Thom Tillis called it 'beyond irresponsible' and said on Tuesday that Putin is 'playing President Trump like a fiddle'; several GOP senators had urged the president to rescind the diesel deal. A national-interest waiver does not repeal the law, so Congress could try to challenge it.

Why it matters: Sanctions policy is now openly tied to domestic fuel prices. Investors should treat future Russia-sanctions decisions as an energy-price tool, which makes them harder to predict.

Sources: Washington Post · The Hill — Tillis

US orders federal civilian agencies to remove DeepSeekAI policy

A DHS order published in the Federal Register requires federal civilian agencies to stop buying DeepSeek products and services and remove them from government IT systems, including third-party products built on DeepSeek technology; agencies may seek waivers. The rationale is the risk that US government user data could be transferred to Chinese authorities. A December 2025 law had already restricted DeepSeek in intelligence and national-security systems.

Separately, DeepSeek open-sourced its DeepInfer inference engine with support for both Nvidia GPUs and Huawei Ascend chips.

Why it matters: The ban formalizes a tech-supply split in public procurement, and DeepSeek's Huawei support shows Chinese AI building a parallel hardware stack — a long-run competitive risk for Nvidia's China exposure.

Sources: TechCrunch · Reuters

France moves toward a 31.4% tax on crypto-to-stablecoin swapsRegulation

France's National Assembly voted narrowly (38–37, 25 abstentions) for a 2027 budget amendment that would make swaps of crypto into MiCA-regulated e-money stablecoins taxable events. Profits would face the 31.4% flat levy. Today, crypto-to-crypto swaps in France defer tax until conversion to fiat. The measure is not law yet and must survive the rest of the budget process; the budget minister opposes it.

Why it matters: Taxing only MiCA-compliant stablecoins would penalize exactly the regulated instruments the EU wants people to use. That is a test case for how European fiscal policy interacts with MiCA.
