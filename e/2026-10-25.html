🧭 The throughline

The market is in a 'financing-regime' correction, not a demand recession: Q3 earnings are growing 29.5% year on year and 83% of reporters are beating, yet the S&P 500 has logged two straight losing weeks because the three things that price AI growth — the cost of debt, the cost of energy and the Fed's reaction function — all moved the wrong way at once. The 10-year sits at a 5% handle, Brent closed Friday at $116.72 (highest since April) on Houthi strikes against Saudi Arabia and US-Iran strike talk, and the Fed meets this week with CPI at 3.7% and a December hike increasingly priced. Capital is now rationed by balance sheet: SpaceX borrowed $40bn on an investment-grade rating while Musk and Jensen Huang both said debt markets have shut to neoclouds, CoreWeave is down 37% in October and Meta's free cash flow went negative for the first time since its IPO. Overlaying all of this is a political calendar — the 3 November midterms — that is pushing the White House toward quick trade 'wins' (Canada 'very close', Mexico 'very close', Audi's $4bn Georgia plant) and toward delaying any Iran escalation, which is why Sunday-night futures rose and oil slipped 2%. The week ahead — Fed Wednesday, Microsoft/Alphabet Wednesday, Amazon Thursday, Q3 GDP and September PCE Thursday, plus Brazil's Monday open after Lula's upset — will decide whether this is a reset of the AI trade's financing or the start of something broader.

🚢 Trade, Tariffs & Policy 4 items

White House swaps 50% tariffs on Canadian goods: 22 products ($5.1bn) exempted, 100 products ($2.6bn) added, effective 30 OctoberGlobal News / BNN Bloomberg

A White House amendment issued Friday 23 October removes the 50% Section 338 tariff from 22 Canadian product lines — corrugated paper and boxes, plastic crates and packing bags, refrigeration and freezing equipment, agricultural machinery, filling/labelling/packing machinery, non-adhesive plastic sheet and film, seaweed products — worth $5.1bn of 2025 imports, and adds 100 new lines at 50% worth $2.6bn, including cocoa powder, paints and varnishes, shampoo, carpets, turf and wooden coffins.

The proclamation frames the tariffs as offsetting 'the burden or disadvantage on U.S. commerce from Canada's discrimination' against US auto exports. The politics are explicit: Senator Susan Collins (R-Maine), tied 49–49 with challenger Troy Jackson, took credit for exemptions 'crucial to Maine's agricultural industries' and said she will seek more; Jackson called it 'a cup of water to put out the fire'; Senate Finance Democrat Ron Wyden said voters are rejecting the tariff policy.

Net effect is a $2.5bn reduction in tariffed trade — a signal, not a settlement — but it tells exporters which way the administration wants the headline to read before 3 November. The Washington Post reports the Canada trade war has become a liability for Republicans in northern border-state races.

Why it matters: this is the first net loosening of the Canada tariff wall since talks collapsed in August; it reads as pre-negotiation goodwill, and the auto-parts lines that remain at 50% are the ones a deal would have to address.

Mexico: Trump and Sheinbaum both say a tariff deal is 'very close'; Mexico wants autos capped at 15% and the 50% metals tariff goneRio Times / Reuters

Trump said Friday 23 October 'We're very close to a deal with Mexico' and that the US holds 'all the cards'; President Claudia Sheinbaum said Tuesday Mexico is 'very close' but that issues remain, with the goal of cutting tariffs on vehicles, steel and aluminium 'to the maximum' and avoiding new ones, and that Mexico will not accept terms that breach its constitution or cost jobs.

Current US tariffs are 50% on steel and aluminium and 25% on cars with exceptions; Mexico wants car tariffs capped at 15% or lower (the Japanese/Korean rate) and a rebate system crediting parts made anywhere in USMCA, and calls the metals tariff unjustified because Mexico is a net steel importer.

USTR opened public comment for the 2027 USMCA review on 5 October; Mexico's consultations begin in January. No text, rates, quotas or signing date have been published — 'very close' is a claim from both sides, not a deal.

Why it matters: a 15% auto cap for Mexico would set the template Canada is negotiating against, and would be the biggest tariff rollback of the year for North American manufacturers — or, if it slips past the midterms, evidence that the deal talk is campaign messaging.

The mechanism is classic tariff-jumping FDI — real jobs in Georgia, paid for by European plant closures and a VW margin collapse — and it lands as Mercedes also pours $4bn into Alabama.

Why it matters: it is the clearest evidence yet that 2025–26 auto tariffs are relocating production rather than just raising prices, which strengthens the administration's hand in the Canada and Mexico auto talks — and weakens Germany's industrial base further.
