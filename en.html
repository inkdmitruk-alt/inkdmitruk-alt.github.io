Treasury rout: 10-year above 5.1%, 30-year near 5.44%, 5-year hits 5% for first time since 2007CNBC / TheStreet

On Wednesday the 10-year yield posted its biggest one-day jump since April 2025, climbing above 5.11% — its highest level since July 2007 — while the 5-year touched 5% for the first time since 2007. On Thursday the 30-year hit roughly 5.44%, a peak last seen in 2004, before easing to about 5.40%; the 10-year pulled back to around 5.10%. Three drivers stacked up: stronger-than-expected economic data, a poorly received Treasury auction, and crude above $100 feeding inflation expectations.

Rising term premium means investors are demanding more to hold long-dated US debt amid heavy supply and sticky inflation. Higher risk-free rates lift the discount rate on all future cash flows, which hits long-duration equities, real estate and utilities hardest.

Why it matters: a sustained 10-year above 5.1% pushes 30-year mortgage rates toward 7.5–8%, freezing housing turnover and tightening financial conditions without any further Fed action.

Sources: CNBC · TheStreet

US–China 'Busan Agreement' extended two months to Jan. 10NBC / Nikkei / CNBC

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said after an unscheduled meeting with Vice Premier He Lifeng that the trade truce struck in South Korea last October, due to expire Nov. 10, will run through Jan. 10. Many had expected an extension of six months or longer ahead of Xi's first state visit to Washington. The short window keeps tariff escalation risk alive into early 2027 and signals that core disputes — chips export controls, rare earths, agricultural purchases — remain unresolved. China also confirmed the first bilateral talks on AI have taken place.

Why it matters: a January cliff means importers and exporters (soybeans, Boeing, semiconductors) must again hedge for a possible tariff snapback, weighing on capex and inventory decisions.

Sources: NBC News · Nikkei Asia · CNBC

H-1B $100,000 entry restriction extended through Sept. 21, 2027Federal Register / White House

A proclamation signed Sept. 18 and published in the Federal Register Sept. 23 extends for 12 months the requirement of a $100,000 payment for new H-1B petitions for workers outside the US. The White House says IT staffing firms cut H-1B registrations by 92% and consular processing requests fell nearly 97% under the original order, while the share of registrants with master's degrees rose from 45.1% to 66.1%.

A companion presidential memo orders tighter interagency oversight of the program. Exemptions remain at the discretion of the Homeland Security Secretary on national-interest grounds.

Why it matters: for tech and outsourcing firms, the cost of offshore-to-onshore talent pipelines stays prohibitive, accelerating nearshoring and offshore delivery models and raising US wage pressure in specialized roles.

Sources: Federal Register · White House
